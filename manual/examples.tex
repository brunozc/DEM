\chapter{Verification and application examples}
\label{cha:examples}

This chapter validates the implementation against closed-form solutions,
quantifies the accuracy of the contact model in a controlled single-collision
experiment, applies the model to the full deposition--compression problem of
Section~\ref{sec:problem}, to two problems with general geometry -- a strip
footing in a trapezoidal container and a curved indenter in a stepped
container -- and finally to submerged columns, fluidized by upward seepage and
consolidated under a sudden load.  Every number reported here was produced
with the code in this repository; the commands needed to reproduce them are
given with each example.

\section{Unit verification against closed-form results}
\label{sec:unit-tests}

The automated test suite is executed with
\begin{verbatim}
  python -m pytest tests/ -q
\end{verbatim}
and contains \num{278} tests, grouped by module, which include checks that
invalid input is rejected with a specific message.  The four examples below each
isolate one equation of Chapter~\ref{cha:formulation}.  Further tests compare
complete simulations with closed-form results: the static Hertz overlap of a
particle resting on a wall, a single contact for a particle on or next to a
vertex shared by two edges, conservation of momentum in an oblique collision,
the equilibrium of a strip footing, of a platen on a side wall and of a
V-shaped body of two edges that touches a particle with its tip, the removal of
particles through an open edge, and the filling of a trapezoidal domain.
The three-dimensional model is checked in the same way: a particle resting on a floor face, a single contact on the edges and the vertex shared by four floor faces and under the tip of a pyramidal body, an oblique collision, rolling on an inclined floor (Section~\ref{sec:3d}), loaded and seated platens, the filling of an extruded trapezoid, and the reproduction of planar simulations by their extrusions.
For the water, the tests check buoyancy, the settling velocity, the porosity $1-\pi/6$ of a simple cubic lattice and the dissipation of an excess pressure.
All tests pass.

\subsection{Example 1 -- free fall of a single body}

\paragraph{Purpose.}  Verify the velocity-Verlet
implementation~\eqref{eq:vv1}--\eqref{eq:vv4}.

\paragraph{Setup.}  One body, no contacts, constant acceleration
$\mathbf{a}=-g\,\mathbf{e}_{y}$ with $g=\qty{9.81}{\metre\per\second\squared}$,
starting from rest at the origin, one step of
$\Delta t=\qty{1e-3}{\second}$.

\paragraph{Reference solution.}  For a constant acceleration the scheme is not
merely second-order accurate but \emph{exact}, because the Taylor expansion of
the trajectory truncates:
\begin{equation}
  y(\Delta t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}g\,\Delta t^{2},
  \qquad
  v_{y}(\Delta t) = -g\,\Delta t .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Result.}  The computed values reproduce
$y=\qty{-4.905e-6}{\metre}$ and $v_{y}=\qty{-9.81e-3}{\metre\per\second}$ to
machine precision, which confirms both the ordering of the kick--drift--kick
stages and the absence of a spurious $\Delta t$ factor.

\subsection{Example 2 -- Hertzian normal force}

\paragraph{Purpose.}  Verify the elastic branch~\eqref{eq:hertz} of the normal
law together with the effective quantities~\eqref{eq:effective}.

\paragraph{Setup.}  Two equal particles, $R=\qty{10}{\milli\metre}$, overlap
$\delta_{n}=\qty{0.1}{\milli\metre}$, zero relative velocity (so that the
dashpot is inactive), and $e=1$ so that $\beta=0$.  The effective radius is
$R^{*}=R/2=\qty{5}{\milli\metre}$.

\paragraph{Reference solution.}
\begin{equation}
  k_{n} = \tfrac{4}{3}E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}}
        = \tfrac{4}{3}\,(\qty{2.6667e6}{\pascal})\sqrt{\qty{5e-3}{\metre}}
        = \qty{2.5142e5}{\newton\per\metre^{3/2}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  F_{n} = k_{n}\,\delta_{n}^{3/2}
        = \qty{2.5142e5}{} \times (\qty{1e-4}{})^{3/2}
        = \qty{0.251416}{\newton} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Result.}  The model returns $F_{x}=\qty{0.2514157}{\newton}$, in
agreement to a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$, with $F_{y}$ and both
tangential components identically zero -- confirming that a purely normal
approach generates no shear.

\subsection{Example 3 -- Coulomb sliding limit}

\paragraph{Purpose.}  Verify the return mapping~\eqref{eq:coulomb}.

\paragraph{Setup.}  The same contact as in Example 2, with $\mu=0.5$ and a
large tangential relative velocity $v_{t}=\qty{5}{\metre\per\second}$ imposed
over $\Delta t=\qty{1e-5}{\second}$.  The tangential stiffness is
$S_{t}=8G^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}\delta_{n}}=\qty{3232.5}{\newton\per\metre}$ and the
spring accumulated in one step is
$\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert=v_{t}\Delta t=\qty{5e-5}{\metre}$, so the trial
spring force alone,
$S_{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert=\qty{0.162}{\newton}$, already exceeds the
friction cone.

\paragraph{Reference solution.}
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\mathbf{F}_{t}\rVert = \mu F_{n}
  = 0.5\times\qty{0.251416}{\newton} = \qty{0.125708}{\newton}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Result.}  The returned tangential force equals $\mu F_{n}$ to within
a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, and never exceeds it.  This confirms both the
scaling of the force onto the cone and the consistency of the spring reset
$\boldsymbol{\xi}=-\mathbf{F}_{t}/S_{t}$.

\subsection{Example 4 -- effective moduli}

\paragraph{Purpose.}  Verify~\eqref{eq:moduli}.

\paragraph{Reference solution.}  For $E=\qty{5}{\mega\pascal}$ and $\nu=0.25$:
$G=E/[2(1+\nu)]=\qty{2.0}{\mega\pascal}$,
$E^{*}=E/[2(1-\nu^{2})]=\qty{2.6667}{\mega\pascal}$ and
$G^{*}=G/[2(2-\nu)]=\qty{0.57143}{\mega\pascal}$.

\paragraph{Result.}  All three constants match, and both effective moduli are
positive, which guards against a sign error in the $(1-\nu^{2})$ or $(2-\nu)$
factors.

\section{Component test: binary collision and restitution}
\label{sec:component-tests}

\subsection{Set-up}

The unit tests above check individual expressions but not the dynamic
behaviour that emerges from their combination.  The classical benchmark for a
soft-sphere contact model is the head-on collision of two identical grains: the
model should return exactly the restitution coefficient $e$ that was prescribed
as an input, and that value should be independent of the impact velocity.

Two particles of radius $R=\qty{10}{\milli\metre}$ of the reference material,
without gravity and \qty{0.1}{\milli\metre} apart, are launched against each
other with relative velocity $v_{\text{rel}}=2v_{0}$ and integrated with the
production solver until they separate.  The measured restitution is
\begin{equation}
  e_{\text{out}}
  = \frac{\lvert v_{\text{rel}}^{\,\text{after}}\rvert}
         {\lvert v_{\text{rel}}^{\,\text{before}}\rvert}.
\end{equation}
A very small time step ($\alpha=0.02$, i.e.\ $\Delta t=T_{R}/50$) is used so
that the integration error is negligible and only the \emph{constitutive} error
is measured.  The results of this section are produced with
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/collision.py
\end{verbatim}

Table~\ref{tab:restitution} supports three conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \textbf{Velocity independence is excellent.}  Doubling the impact
        velocity changes $e_{\text{out}}$ by less than $0.1\%$.  This is the
        property that the Tsuji scaling $c_{n}\propto\sqrt{S_{n}m^{*}}$ was
        designed to deliver, and it is reproduced essentially exactly.  A model
        with a constant (velocity-independent) dashpot would show a strong
        drift here.
  \item \textbf{There is a systematic positive bias in $e$.}  The measured
        restitution always exceeds the prescribed one, and the discrepancy grows
        rapidly as $e$ decreases: negligible at $e=0.9$, $10\%$ at $e=0.5$, and
        $62\%$ at $e=0.2$.  The cause is well known and is not an implementation
        error: relation~\eqref{eq:beta} is the exact solution for a
        \emph{linear} spring--dashpot oscillator, whereas the contact here is
        Hertzian, with a stiffness that vanishes at the beginning and at the end
        of the collision.  The dashpot is therefore weak exactly where the
        linear theory assumes it to be strong, and less energy is
        dissipated~\cite{direnzo2004,thornton2013}.
  \item \textbf{Practical consequence.}  The input $e$ should be regarded as a
        \emph{damping parameter}, not as a directly measurable restitution
        coefficient.  If a specific restitution must be reproduced,
        Table~\ref{tab:restitution} can be read backwards: interpolating
        between the rows $e=0.2$ and $e=0.4$, a physical $e\approx0.4$ requires
        an input of about $0.3$.  The reference simulation uses $e=0.4$ as an
        input, which corresponds to an effective restitution of about $0.47$.
\end{enumerate}

isolates the \emph{integration} error from the constitutive error.

The measured restitution converges monotonically to $0.4715$.  Refining the

Four observations deserve comment.

\paragraph{The realised size distribution matches the input.}  Radii are drawn
from $\mathcal{U}(\qty{4}{\milli\metre},\qty{20}{\milli\metre})$, whose mean is
\qty{12}{\milli\metre} and whose standard deviation is
\qty{4.62}{\milli\metre}; the deposited assembly has \qty{12.25}{\milli\metre}
and \qty{4.67}{\milli\metre}.  Each eighth of the radius range should hold
\num{22.4} particles on average, and the assembly has \num{22} particles below
\qty{6}{\milli\metre} and \num{26} above \qty{18}{\milli\metre}.  This is the
result of drawing each radius once and retrying it until it is placed
(Section~\ref{sec:deposition}): the first version of the code drew a new radius
at every placement attempt, which favoured small grains and gave a mean radius
of only \qty{7.6}{\milli\metre} and \num{460} particles in the same box.

\paragraph{The packing is dense and well connected.}  An area fraction of
\num{0.828} and a coordination number of \num{3.55} are typical of a
polydisperse two-dimensional random packing.  A value of $Z$ above the
isostatic threshold for frictional discs ($Z=3$) confirms that the assembly is
mechanically stable rather than marginally rigid.  The box is only about eight
mean diameters wide, and the layers along a flat wall pack less densely than the
interior, so the area fraction of a wider box would be somewhat higher.

\paragraph{The overlaps are already large under self-weight.}  A mean
$\delta_{n}/R$ of \num{2.8}\% and a maximum of \num{11.3}\% before any external
load is applied are well above the one per cent guideline
of~\eqref{eq:overlap-criterion}.  It is a direct consequence of the artificial
softening.  The floor carries \qty{37.7}{\newton} through only seven contacts,
\qty{5.4}{\newton} each on average, and the estimate~\eqref{eq:delta-static}
with that force and $R^{*}=\bar{R}$ gives $\delta_{n}=\qty{0.57}{\milli\metre}$,
i.e.\ \num{4.7}\% of $\bar{R}$ at the bottom of the bed, where the contact
forces are largest.  The deposited packing should therefore be regarded as
qualitatively, not quantitatively, representative; raising $E$ is the remedy.

\paragraph{The walls carry part of the weight.}  The floor carries only
\num{78}\% of the weight of the particles; the remaining \num{22}\% is
transferred to the side walls by friction.  This is the Janssen effect, and it
is already present under self-weight in a container this slender.

\subsection{Compression stage}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/load_displacement_1000N.png}
  \caption{Reference simulation, $F_{\max}=\qty{1000}{\newton}$.  Left: applied
  load $F(t)$ and contact reaction $F_{p}$ against loading time.  Right:
  reaction against platen displacement.}
  \label{fig:loadcurve1000}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:loadcurve1000} shows the response.  The applied load ramps
linearly to \qty{1000}{\newton} over \qty{0.4}{\second} and is then held for a
further \qty{0.6}{\second}.  The contact reaction $F_{p}$ tracks $F(t)$ closely,
within \qty{24}{\newton} (\num{2.4}\% of the peak) during the ramp, and during
the hold it averages \qty{999.97}{\newton} with a standard deviation of
\qty{0.85}{\newton}; at the end it is \qty{999.98}{\newton}.  This is the
system-level consistency check announced in Section~\ref{sec:verification}: at
equilibrium Eq.~\eqref{eq:platen-eom} requires $F_{p}=F(t)$, and the residual
measures how far the platen still is from static equilibrium.

The final platen displacement is $u_{p}=\qty{131.6}{\milli\metre}$, i.e.\ a
nominal axial strain of \num{22.5}\% of the bed height at seating,
\qty{0.584}{\metre}.  Such a strain cannot be produced by grain rearrangement
alone in a packing that is already dense; it is produced by interpenetration.
The twelve particles under the platen overlap it by \num{42.5}\% of their radius
on average and by up to \num{76}\%, and the mean particle--particle overlap has
grown to \num{22}\%; Figure~\ref{fig:snapshots}(c) shows the grains visibly
merging.  This case is therefore \emph{outside} the validity range of the
Hertz--Mindlin law and is retained here as a deliberate illustration of what
excessive loading looks like.

Most of the load does not reach the floor.  Of the \qty{1048.5}{\newton} of
applied load and weight, the floor carries \qty{458}{\newton}, \num{44}\%; the
side walls carry the other \num{56}\% by friction.  The measured platen
reaction is therefore a property of the packing \emph{and} of the walls, as
anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Effect of the load level}
\label{sec:load-level}

To quantify the previous statement, the identical packing (same seed, same
deposition history) was compressed with a peak load of \qty{100}{\newton}
instead of \qty{1000}{\newton}; the case is reproduced by setting
\texttt{amplitude=100.0} in the \texttt{Heaviside} load of
\texttt{build\_simulation}.  The result is
Figure~\ref{fig:loadcurve100} and Table~\ref{tab:loadlevels}.

A tenfold increase of the load produces only a \num{5.0}-fold increase of the
displacement, so the packing stiffens as it is compressed.  Qualitatively this
is the expected signature of a Hertzian assembly: from~\eqref{eq:hertz} a single
contact obeys $F\propto\delta^{3/2}$, hence a secant stiffness scaling as
$F^{1/3}$, i.e.\ a factor $10^{1/3}=\num{2.15}$ between the two load levels.
The measured ratio, $7.60/3.81=\num{1.99}$, is \num{7}\% below this value.  The
agreement should not be over-interpreted: the \qty{1000}{\newton} case is far
outside the small-overlap regime in which~\eqref{eq:hertz} was derived, both
cases include irreversible rearrangement of the contact network, and in both the
side walls carry most of the load.  The comparison indicates a predominantly
Hertzian stiffening rather than validating the exponent.  The measured overlaps
under the platen, \num{8.7}\% and \num{42.5}\% of the radius, agree well with
the a priori estimates of Table~\ref{tab:overlap} (\num{10.6}\% and
\num{49.2}\%).

The detailed history of the \qty{100}{\newton} case is reproduced in
Table~\ref{tab:loadhistory}.  The reaction stays within \qty{3.6}{\newton} of
the applied load throughout the ramp, and during the hold
($t\ge\qty{0.5}{\second}$) it averages $\qty{100.00}{\newton}$ with a standard
deviation of only $\qty{0.09}{\newton}$.  The loading is therefore quasi-static
in the sense that inertial effects are negligible, which is precisely the regime
in which an oedometric test should be interpreted.

\section{Strip footing in a trapezoidal container}
\label{sec:footing}

The script \texttt{examples/footing\_on\_trapezoid.py} exercises the general
input: a non-rectangular domain, a different boundary condition on the edges, a
load applied to part of a side only, and a choice of load histories.  The
container is the trapezoid with vertices $(0.05,0)$, $(0.35,0)$, $(0.40,0.25)$
and $(0,0.25)$~\unit{\metre}, which widens upwards.  Its bottom and left side
are rough walls and its right side is a smooth wall ($\mu_{w}=0$).  The top side
is split into three collinear edges: the outer two are open, and the middle one,
\qty{0.1}{\metre} wide, is a loaded platen of mass \qty{0.1}{\kilogram} that acts
as a rigid strip footing.  The footing has self weight (\qty{0.98}{\newton}), so
that it rests on the fill whenever the load is removed; a weightless body under
zero load would be free, and the elastic rebound of the fill would send it away.
\begin{verbatim}
  vertices = [(0.05, 0.0), (0.35, 0.0), (0.40, 0.25),
              (0.25, 0.25), (0.15, 0.25), (0.00, 0.25)]
  footing = LoadedPlaten(load=load, mass=0.1, self_weight=True)
  boundary_conditions = [Wall(), Wall(friction=0.0), Open(), footing,
                         Open(), Wall()]
  geometry = Geometry(vertices, boundary_conditions)
\end{verbatim}
The container is rained full with radii in $[4,8]$~\unit{\milli\metre} up to an
insertion band at $y\in[0.19,0.20]$~\unit{\metre}, which gives \num{482}
particles and a bed top at \qty{0.205}{\metre}; when loading starts the footing
is lowered by \qty{44.5}{\milli\metre} onto the fill.  A command-line argument
selects one of four load histories -- a pulse, a Heaviside step, a sinusoid, or
a time history that loads, partly unloads and reloads -- with peaks of
\qty{4}{\newton}.  In all four runs the settlement at the end of the
\qty{1}{\second} loading stage is \qtyrange{5}{12}{\milli\metre}, and the final
reaction equals the final load plus the weight of the footing to within
\qty{0.15}{\newton}.

These levels were chosen after loading the footing with a slow ramp to
\qty{20}{\newton} over \qty{2}{\second} (Table~\ref{tab:footing}, produced with
\texttt{python manual/scripts/footing.py}).  The settlement grows steadily to
\qty{12}{\milli\metre} at \qty{8}{\newton} of applied load.  Between
\qty{8}{\newton} and \qty{10}{\newton} it jumps to \qty{52}{\milli\metre}, and
the footing punches into the fill while material flows around its ends and closes
over it (Figure~\ref{fig:footing}); it is arrested again at about
\qty{67}{\milli\metre}, beyond which the settlement grows by only
\qty{2}{\milli\metre} up to \qty{18}{\newton}.  The bearing capacity is
therefore about \qty{9}{\newton} to \qty{11}{\newton} including the weight of the
footing, and the example loads, with the weight, stay at about half of it.  A
capacity this low is expected from soft, frictional discs without rolling
resistance (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).  Being a single realisation, the
failure load is only indicative: a different seed changes the details of the
packing under the footing.

\section{Curved indenter in a stepped container}
\label{sec:indenter}

The script \texttt{examples/curved\_indenter.py} combines a more complex domain
with a load that does not act along a straight line.  The container
(Figure~\ref{fig:indenter}a) has a stepped floor -- an upper level at
$y=\qty{0.06}{\metre}$ for $x<\qty{0.12}{\metre}$ and a lower level at $y=0$ --
whose upper corner is a re-entrant vertex, a vertical left wall, and a right
wall inclined from $(0.30,0)$ to $(0.38,0.30)$~\unit{\metre} with a friction
coefficient of $\mu_{w}=0.2$.  The top is open except for the indenter, a rigid
shell whose lower surface is an arc of a circle of radius \qty{70}{\milli\metre}
centred at $(0.19,0.34)$~\unit{\metre}; the arc cuts the top of the container
and dips \qty{30}{\milli\metre} below it.  The arc is approximated by eight
edges that all carry the same \texttt{LoadedPlaten}, so they form a single body
of mass \qty{0.1}{\kilogram}, with self weight, pushed vertically:
\begin{verbatim}
  indenter = LoadedPlaten(load=load, mass=0.1, self_weight=True,
                          direction=(0.0, -1.0))
  vertices = [(0.00, 0.06), (0.12, 0.06), (0.12, 0.00), (0.30, 0.00),
              (0.38, 0.30), *indenter_arc(), (0.00, 0.30)]
  boundary_conditions = [Wall(), Wall(), Wall(), Wall(friction=0.2), Open(),
                         *[indenter] * 8, Open(), Wall()]
\end{verbatim}
Without the explicit direction the body would move vertically as well, because
the length-weighted normals~\eqref{eq:platen-direction} of the symmetric arc add
up to $(0,-1)$.

The container is rained full with radii in $[3,6]$~\unit{\milli\metre} up to an
insertion band at $y\in[0.22,0.23]$~\unit{\metre}, which gives \num{850}
particles and a bed top at \qty{0.234}{\metre}; seating lowers the indenter by
\qty{35.8}{\milli\metre} until its lowest point touches the fill.  The results
are produced with
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/indenter.py
\end{verbatim}
which loads two copies of this packing.

\paragraph{Capacity.}  Under a slow ramp of the applied load to
\qty{20}{\newton} over \qty{2}{\second} (Table~\ref{tab:indenter}) the response
is soft at first, while only the lowest part of the arc bears on the irregular
surface: \qty{20}{\milli\metre} of settlement at \qty{1}{\newton} and
\qty{37}{\milli\metre} at \qty{4}{\newton}, by which point the
\qty{30}{\milli\metre} deep arc is fully embedded.  The response is then much
stiffer, and the settlement grows by only \qty{4}{\milli\metre} up to
\qty{8}{\newton}.  Between \qty{8}{\newton} and \qty{10}{\newton} the fill fails:
the settlement jumps to \qty{88}{\milli\metre} at \qty{10}{\newton} and
\qty{153}{\milli\metre} at \qty{14}{\newton}, after which the indenter, now
buried with about \qty{8}{\centi\metre} of fill left above the lower floor, is
arrested and moves only \qty{2}{\milli\metre} more up to \qty{20}{\newton}
(Figure~\ref{fig:indenter-ramp}).  As for the footing, the failure load of a
single realisation is only indicative.

\subsection{Verification}

The test suite checks the water model against closed-form results: a submerged
particle resting on a wall presses on it with its buoyant weight, a particle of
half the density of water floats with its centre at the free surface, a rising
water table reduces the contact force of a resting particle from its weight to
its buoyant weight, the drag tends to \num{96}\% of Stokes' law at vanishing
Reynolds number, a square lattice of discs has porosity $1-\pi/4$ also next to
the walls, and an upward flow lifts a single particle once its superficial
velocity exceeds the particle's settling velocity.  In water \num{e5} times more
viscous, where the drag would make an explicit step unstable, a particle settles
at the default time step exactly at its Stokes-regime terminal velocity.  For
coupled water, the pressure solver reproduces Terzaghi's dissipation of a
uniform excess pressure to within \num{1}\% of its initial value, a column under
a sudden load passes it from the water to the skeleton as the excess pressure
dissipates, and a particle settling alone in clear water is unaffected by the
coupling.

The settling of single particles over a wide range of sizes is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:settling}, produced with
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/water.py
\end{verbatim}
A sphere of density \qty{2500}{\kilogram\per\cubic\metre} is released from rest
in still water and its speed after a few acceleration times is compared with the
terminal velocity at which the flow force~\eqref{eq:interaction} balances its
buoyant weight.
The two agree to better than \num{0.02}\% from a Reynolds number of \num{0.7} to
\num{6400}, which verifies buoyancy, drag and their time integration together.
The Stokes velocity $2(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})gR^{2}/(9\mu_{w})$ is \num{18}\% too
high already for the finest grains, and overestimates the settling of millimetre
grains by more than an order of magnitude: at those sizes the drag is dominated
by inertia, which the correlation captures.

\subsection{Upward seepage: from the quick condition to fluidization}

The script \texttt{examples/upward\_seepage.py} reproduces the classical
permeameter experiment.  A box \qty{0.2}{\metre} wide and \qty{0.5}{\metre} high,
with a rough floor, smooth side walls ($\mu_{w}=0$, so that wall friction does
not carry part of the bed) and an open top, is filled with water up to
$y_{w}=\qty{0.45}{\metre}$.  Particles of the reference material with radii in
$[4,6]$~\unit{\milli\metre} are rained into the water up to an insertion band at
$y\in[0.24,0.25]$~\unit{\metre}, which gives \num{502} particles, a bed top at
\qty{0.252}{\metre} and a mean porosity of \num{0.395}.  An upward flow is then
ramped linearly from zero to twice the minimum fluidization
velocity~\eqref{eq:umf} of the settled bed, $U_{mf}=\qty{0.0634}{\metre\per\second}$,
over \qty{4}{\second}:
\begin{verbatim}
  start = sim.time
  sim.set_water(Water(level=0.45, seepage_velocity=TimeHistory(
      [start, start + 4.0], [0.0, 2.0 * u_mf])))
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Effective stress at rest.}  Before the flow starts, the force of the
bed on the floor, averaged over \qty{0.1}{\second}, is
$\qty{4.071}{\newton}\pm\qty{0.025}{\newton}$, against a buoyant weight of
\qty{4.070}{\newton}: the floor carries exactly the buoyant weight, \num{60}\% of
the dry weight of \qty{6.783}{\newton}.  The contact forces are the effective
forces, and the water carries the rest.

\paragraph{Fluidization velocity.}  For the same mean porosity and Sauter mean
diameter, Ergun's equation for the pressure drop through a packed bed gives a
minimum fluidization velocity of \qty{0.0707}{\metre\per\second}; the model's
fixed-bed value is \num{10}\% lower.  Ergun's equation is an independent,
empirical description of packed beds, so this agreement checks the flow
force~\eqref{eq:interaction} as a whole, including the pressure gradient that it
adds to the drag: with the drag alone, the force on this bed would be smaller by
the factor $\varepsilon\approx0.4$ and $U_{mf}$ correspondingly higher.

\paragraph{Fluidization.}  Figure~\ref{fig:fluidization} shows how the force on
the floor, normalised by the buoyant weight, falls as the flow increases, and
compares it with the fixed-bed estimate $1-\sum F^{\,f}/W'$, the force that the
same flow would exert on the particles if they did not move.  Up to half of
$U_{mf}$ the two agree to within \num{1}\% of $W'$ (Table~\ref{tab:fluidization}).
Closer to $U_{mf}$ the floor carries more than the estimate: as its effective
weight decreases the bed dilates slightly, its porosity rising from \num{0.395}
to \num{0.403} at $U_{mf}$, and the flow force on the looser bed is lower.  The
force on the floor falls to \num{2}\% of $W'$ at about $1.1\,U_{mf}$, when the bed
starts to lift; a residual of a few per cent, from particles wedged against the
floor, persists up to $1.5\,U_{mf}$.  Beyond fluidization the bed expands
steadily: its top rises from \qty{0.252}{\metre} to \qty{0.297}{\metre} at
$2\,U_{mf}$ and its mean porosity grows to \num{0.51}.  The expanded bed is not
uniform: particles circulate and its surface domes
(Figure~\ref{fig:seepage-snapshots}), the onset of the heterogeneous flow of real
fluidized beds.  No particle is carried out of the column, since even the
smallest settle alone at \qty{0.56}{\metre\per\second}, \num{4.5} times the
largest flow.

\subsection{Undrained loading and consolidation}
\label{sec:consolidation}

The script \texttt{examples/consolidation.py} reproduces the oedometer test of a
saturated soil.  A box \qty{0.2}{\metre} wide, with a rough, impermeable floor and
smooth side walls, holds water up to $y_{w}=\qty{0.38}{\metre}$; its top edge is a
loaded platen of mass \qty{0.5}{\kilogram}, which lets the water through like a
porous stone (Figure~\ref{fig:consolidation-column}).  Particles of the reference
material with radii in $[4,6]$~\unit{\milli\metre} are rained into drained water
up to an insertion band at $y\in[0.20,0.21]$~\unit{\metre}, which gives \num{423}
particles, a column of height $H=\qty{0.213}{\metre}$ and a mean porosity of
\num{0.401}.  The water is then coupled and made a thousand times more viscous
than water, $\mu_{w}=\qty{1}{\pascal\second}$, so that these grains drain like a
soil a thousand times less permeable, and a load of \qty{20}{\newton} is applied in
\qty{0.01}{\second}:
\begin{verbatim}
  sim.set_water(Water(level=0.38, coupled=True, viscosity=1.0))
  sim.compress(3.0)
\end{verbatim}
With $K_{w}=\qty{10}{\mega\pascal}$ the coupling limits the time step to
\qty{6.7e-5}{\second} (Section~\ref{sec:water-numerics}), and the run takes about
\qty{100}{\second}.  The results are produced with
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/pore_pressure.py
\end{verbatim}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/consolidation_column.png}
  \caption{The column at the end of the consolidation test: water (blue) up to
  the water table, the loaded platen (red) on top of the column.}
  \label{fig:consolidation-column}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Undrained response.}  Before loading, the floor carries
\qty{3.39}{\newton}, \num{99}\% of the buoyant weight of the column.  When the
load is applied the skeleton cannot compress without expelling water, and the
water takes the load: \qty{0.06}{\second} after loading the floor has received
only \num{1.6}\% of it.  For a moment the platen, pushing on almost
incompressible water, makes the pressure ring, with a peak of
\qty{16.5}{\kilo\pascal} at the base; after \qty{0.1}{\second} the pressure decays
smoothly.  Fitted from \qty{0.15}{\second} on, the initial excess pressure of
Terzaghi's theory is $p_{0}=\qty{12.1}{\kilo\pascal}$, \num{12}\% above the load
divided by the area of mixture it acts on, $FH/\sum_{i}V_{i}/(1-\varepsilon_{i})
=\qty{10.7}{\kilo\pascal}$, an estimate that ignores the variation of porosity
along the column.  With the constrained modulus measured below, Skempton's
coefficient is $B=1/(1+\varepsilon M/K_{w})=\num{0.988}$: the scaled bulk modulus of
the water is still large enough for a practically undrained response.

\paragraph{Consolidation.}  The excess pressure then dissipates through the top
of the column, and the load passes from the water to the skeleton
(Figure~\ref{fig:consolidation}, Table~\ref{tab:consolidation}).  Fitting
Terzaghi's base pressure for the coefficient of consolidation gives
$c_{v}=\qty{0.0265}{\metre\squared\per\second}$, with which the base pressure, the
share of the load on the floor and the settlement follow the theory closely.  The
fitted coefficient can be checked against a prediction from the ingredients of
the model.  The settlement during consolidation, \qty{7.9}{\milli\metre}, gives a
constrained modulus $M=p_{0}H/s_{c}=\qty{0.33}{\mega\pascal}$, and with the
permeability~\eqref{eq:permeability} at the mean porosity and Sauter radius of
the column, $c_{v}=(k/\mu_{w})/(1/M+\varepsilon/K_{w})=\qty{0.0247}{\metre\squared\per\second}$,
\num{7}\% below the fitted value.  The pressure profiles
(Figure~\ref{fig:consolidation}, right) show the one systematic difference: the
column drains faster near its top than Terzaghi's uniform layer, because the
packing is looser there and the permeability grows steeply with the porosity,
as $\varepsilon^{4.7}/(1-\varepsilon)$.  Of the final settlement of
\qty{12.6}{\milli\metre}, \qty{4.9}{\milli\metre} occur immediately -- the
seating of the platen and the compression of the water -- and the rest during
consolidation.

\section{Railway ballast under cyclic loading}
\label{sec:ballast}

The script \texttt{examples/ballast\_cyclic.py} asks how the presence of water
changes the response of railway ballast to traffic.  The model is a
longitudinal section through the track that holds one sleeper bay
(Figure~\ref{fig:ballast-snapshots}): a sleeper with a base
\qty{0.25}{\metre} wide rests on \qty{0.3}{\metre} of ballast on a rigid
formation, and ballast fills the cribs on both sides of it.  The side walls are
smooth planes of symmetry halfway to the neighbouring sleepers, so the box is
one sleeper spacing, \qty{0.6}{\metre}, wide.  The grains have radii in
$[15,25]$~\unit{\milli\metre}, the size of real ballast, and are made of a
granite-like material: $\rho=\qty{2650}{\kilogram\per\cubic\metre}$,
$E=\qty{5}{\giga\pascal}$ (ten times softer than granite, to keep the time
step at \qty{1.18e-5}{\second}), $\nu=0.25$, $e=0.5$, $\mu=0.7$ and a rolling
friction $\mu_{r}=0.3$.  The sleeper is a \texttt{LoadedPlaten} on three edges
-- its base and its two sides, which reach the top of the box so that crib
ballast cannot spill onto it -- with a mass of \qty{4}{\kilogram}, the share of
a \qty{300}{\kilogram} sleeper in a slice as thick as a grain, and its own
weight.

\paragraph{Rolling resistance is essential.}  Round grains are far too weak for
track loads.  In a preliminary calibration (one packing, $E=\qty{0.5}{\giga\pascal}$,
ten cycles of the load below) the sleeper punched \qty{165}{\milli\metre} into
the bed without rolling resistance, and settled by \qtylist{20;4.2;1.5}{\milli\metre}
with $\mu_{r}=0.15$, $0.3$ and $0.5$.  With $\mu_{r}=0.3$ the bed shows the
behaviour of real ballast, a large first cycle followed by a decaying rate of
settlement, so that value is used here.  It is a calibration, not a measured
property (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Procedure.}  The ballast is deposited dry in two passes.  The first
fills the bed under the sleeper, which is then lowered onto it with
\texttt{seat\_platens()} -- seated but not yet released -- and the second fills
the cribs around it.  The bed ends with some \num{140} to \num{150} grains, the
sleeper base \qtyrange{0.275}{0.284}{\metre} above the formation and the cribs
about \qty{0.51}{\metre} high.  Each packing is then loaded in three cases:
dry; with the water table halfway down the ballast under the sleeper; and
saturated, with the water at the top of the cribs.  After \qty{0.3}{\second} of
rest the sleeper receives \num{50} cycles of a \qty{5}{\hertz} load from zero
to \qty{2}{\kilo\newton}.  Dividing the load by the base width and by the
thickness of the slice, the volume of mixture over the area of the bed, gives a
peak pressure of \qty{207}{\kilo\pascal} on the sleeper base, typical of
heavily loaded track.  Because a bed of this size depends on its packing, five
packings are deposited, and all fifteen runs are made in parallel:
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/ballast.py
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Drained water.}  Clean ballast is extremely permeable.  With the
permeability~\eqref{eq:permeability} of these grains, $k\approx\qty{2e-6}{\metre\squared}$,
and a constrained modulus of the bed of order \qty{40}{\mega\pascal}, the
coefficient of consolidation is of order \qty{e4}{\metre\squared\per\second},
so the bed drains in microseconds, against a loading period of
\qty{0.2}{\second}.  The water is therefore drained: it only buoys the grains
below the water table and drags on them when they move.  A coupled run of the
saturated case over two cycles confirms it: the largest excess pore pressure
under the sleeper is \qty{4.6}{\pascal}, \num{0.002}\% of the pressure on the
base.

\paragraph{Results.}  Table~\ref{tab:ballast} and Figure~\ref{fig:ballast}
summarise the runs.  In every case the settlement does not grow smoothly: it
advances in sudden steps of \qtyrange{2}{8}{\milli\metre}, each the collapse of
a grain arrangement under the sleeper, separated by long stretches of slow
creep.  With only six grains across the sleeper base, every such event is
large, and when it happens is chaotic: the three cases of one packing, which
start from the same state, collapse at different cycles.  The scatter between
packings, \qty{\pm 4.5}{\milli\metre} after \num{50} cycles, is therefore larger
than any difference between the cases, and the order of the cases changes from
packing to packing (Figure~\ref{fig:ballast}, top right).  Comparing the cases
packing by packing removes the scatter between packings, and leaves one hint: the
saturated bed settled more than the dry one in four packings out of five, by
\qty{1.0}{\milli\metre} on average with a standard deviation of
\qty{1.0}{\milli\metre}, about a tenth of the settlement.  A paired $t$-test gives
$p=0.10$, so the difference is not significant; confirming one of this size would
take some ten packings.  The water table at half depth shows no consistent
trend.  The resilient displacement, about \qty{1.5}{\milli\metre} per cycle, is
the same in all cases within its scatter.

\paragraph{Why the water makes so little difference.}  The only mechanical effect
of drained water on a clean, static bed is buoyancy, and buoyancy changes only
the stresses due to the weight of the grains.  For the saturated bed it takes
49.8~\unit{\newton} off the weight of the ballast, \num{2.5}\% of the
peak sleeper load; the effective vertical stress of the crib ballast at the
level of the sleeper base falls from about \qty{3.7}{\kilo\pascal} to
\qty{2.3}{\kilo\pascal}, against \qty{207}{\kilo\pascal} under the sleeper.
That weight confines the ballast under the sleeper and resists its spreading
into the cribs, so if the hint above is real, it has the expected sign.  The
drag is negligible too: the grains under the sleeper move at most at about
\qty{2}{\centi\metre\per\second}, at which the drag on a grain is below one per
cent of its weight.  The
model thus predicts that water in clean, free-draining ballast changes the
settlement by at most about a tenth, less than the variability of the packing.
This agrees with the view of track engineering that clean ballast is not
weakened by water itself; the damage that water does to track comes from
fouled ballast, which drains slowly and lets fines be pumped up, and from a
saturated, softened subgrade~\cite{selig1994}.  None of those mechanisms is in
this model: the formation is rigid, the ballast has no fines, and wet grain
surfaces have the same friction as dry ones and do not break.  The suggested
studies below list how some of them could be added.

\section{Three-dimensional examples}
\label{sec:examples-3d}

Four scripts in \texttt{examples/} exercise the three-dimensional model of Section~\ref{sec:3d}.
Each runs in one to three minutes and writes an animation in an oblique view, which a PDF cannot show; the animations are kept in \texttt{examples/videos/}, where the README shows them.
The figures of this section and the animations are produced with
\begin{verbatim}
  python manual/scripts/examples_3d.py
\end{verbatim}
The examples share the material of the reference simulation, $E=\qty{5}{\mega\pascal}$, $\nu=0.25$, $e=0.4$ and $\mu=0.5$, with a rolling friction $\mu_{r}=0.1$ for the three dry ones, and are filled by raining.
Figures~\ref{fig:examples-3d-sections} and~\ref{fig:examples-3d-hopper} show vertical sections through three of them.

\subsection{Square footing}

The script \texttt{examples/square\_footing\_3d.py} loads a rigid square footing in the middle of a granular bed, a problem without a planar counterpart.
The box is \qty{0.2}{\metre} wide and deep and \qty{0.15}{\metre} high, with a rough floor and smooth sides.
Its top is split into five faces: the footing, a square of side \qty{70}{\milli\metre} that carries a \texttt{LoadedPlaten} of mass \qty{0.2}{\kilogram} with self weight, and four open trapezoids around it.
The box is rained full with \num{552} particles of radius \qtyrange{8}{12}{\milli\metre}, to a bed top at \qty{0.119}{\metre}.
The footing is seated on the bed, and the load is ramped to \qty{15}{\newton} in \qty{0.4}{\second}, held for \qty{0.2}{\second} and removed in \qty{0.2}{\second}.
The reaction follows the load plus the weight of the footing throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:examples-3d-curves}).
The footing settles \qty{7.4}{\milli\metre} by the time the load reaches its peak and \qty{8.0}{\milli\metre} by the end of the hold.
Once the load is removed it keeps \qty{6.2}{\milli\metre}, and its reaction, \qty{1.97}{\newton}, balances its weight of \qty{1.96}{\newton}.

\subsection{Cone penetration}

The script \texttt{examples/cone\_penetration\_3d.py} pushes a rigid cone into the fill of a box \qty{0.16}{\metre} wide and deep and \qty{0.2}{\metre} high.
The cone has an apex angle of \ang{60} and a base of radius \qty{30}{\milli\metre} in the top of the box, so it is \qty{52}{\milli\metre} high.
Its surface is approximated by twelve triangular faces that meet at its tip and carry the same \texttt{LoadedPlaten}, of mass \qty{0.3}{\kilogram} with self weight.
The rest of the top, a square with a dodecagonal hole, is split into two non-convex faces, each around one half of the hole.
The box is rained full with \num{708} particles of radius \qtyrange{7}{10}{\milli\metre}, to a bed top at \qty{0.137}{\metre}; the cone is seated on the fill with its tip, and the load is ramped to \qty{20}{\newton} in \qty{0.8}{\second} and held.
Under its weight of \qty{2.94}{\newton} and the first few newtons of load the cone pushes the loose surface grains aside and sinks quickly, by \qty{17}{\milli\metre} at \qty{2}{\newton} and \qty{36}{\milli\metre} at \qty{4}{\newton}, two thirds of its height (Figure~\ref{fig:examples-3d-curves}).
The resistance then rises steeply as the cone becomes embedded up to its base: it penetrates only \qty{9}{\milli\metre} more up to \qty{20}{\newton}, \qty{45}{\milli\metre} in all (Figure~\ref{fig:examples-3d-sections}).

\subsection{Hopper discharge}

The script \texttt{examples/hopper\_discharge\_3d.py} drains a bin through a hopper.
The bin is \qty{0.12}{\metre} square and \qty{0.24}{\metre} high, and its lower \qty{50}{\milli\metre} narrow into a hopper with a square outlet of side \qty{70}{\milli\metre}, about five particle diameters.
The outlet is a trap door: a \texttt{LoadedPlaten} of \qty{50}{\gram} with self weight and no seating, which holds the grains while the bin is filled.
Once \texttt{compress} releases it, a load of \qty{5}{\newton} pulls it down, far faster than the grains can follow, and grains that leave through the outlet fall out of the domain and are removed, as through any open face.
The bin is rained full with \num{1044} particles of radius \qtyrange{6}{8}{\milli\metre}, \qty{3.85}{\kilo\gram} of grains, to a bed top at \qty{0.216}{\metre}.
In the first second after the door opens, \qty{2.33}{\kilo\gram} of grains, \num{61}\% of them, drain out at a nearly constant rate: \qty{2.49}{\kilo\gram\per\second} between \num{10} and \num{80}\% of that mass (Figure~\ref{fig:examples-3d-hopper}).
The rate does not fall as the bin empties.
A granular bed drains at a rate set by its outlet, not by its head, unlike a liquid: arches that form and collapse over the outlet carry the weight of the grains above it.

\subsection{Upward seepage}

The script \texttt{examples/upward\_seepage\_3d.py} is the three-dimensional counterpart of the seepage example of Section~\ref{sec:water-examples}.
A permeameter \qty{80}{\milli\metre} square and \qty{0.3}{\metre} high, with smooth side walls and the water table at \qty{0.27}{\metre}, is rained full under water with \num{572} spheres of radius \qtyrange{5}{7}{\milli\metre}, without rolling resistance.
The bed top lies at \qty{0.153}{\metre} and the mean porosity is \num{0.46}.
At rest the floor carries the buoyant weight of the bed, \qty{7.72}{\newton} against \qty{7.71}{\newton}.
The force of a seepage flow on the fixed bed equals that buoyant weight at the minimum fluidization velocity $U_{mf}=\qty{0.1025}{\metre\per\second}$, and the flow is then ramped to $2U_{mf}$ in \qty{3}{\second}.
The force on the floor follows the fixed-bed prediction $1-F_{f}/W'$ up to $U_{mf}$ and nearly vanishes beyond it, where the bed lifts and expands, from \qty{153}{\milli\metre} to \qty{199}{\milli\metre} at $2U_{mf}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:examples-3d-seepage}).

\section{Suggested further studies}
\label{sec:further}

The examples above exercise every part of the code, but a number of studies are
natural next steps and are directly supported by the existing interfaces.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \textbf{Softening convergence.}  Repeat the \qty{100}{\newton} case with
        $E=5$, $50$ and \qty{500}{\mega\pascal}.  The time step falls as
        $E^{-1/2}$ and the overlaps as $E^{-2/3}$; the load--displacement curve
        should converge once the overlaps drop below one per cent.  This is the
        single most important study before any quantitative use of the model.
  \item \textbf{Time-step refinement.}  Halve $\alpha$ in~\eqref{eq:dt} and
        verify that the load--displacement curve is unchanged within the
        seed-to-seed scatter.
  \item \textbf{Statistical scatter.}  Repeat the reference case with several
        values of \texttt{seed}.  Individual force chains are chaotic, but the
        bed height, the area fraction and the load--displacement curve should
        vary by only a few per cent.
  \item \textbf{Friction sensitivity.}  Vary $\mu$ between $0$ and $1$.  The
        area fraction should decrease with $\mu$ (friction supports looser
        packings) and the compressive stiffness should increase, because wall
        friction carries a growing share of the load.
  \item \textbf{Unloading.}  Replace the ramped Heaviside
        load~\eqref{eq:ramp} by a \texttt{TimeHistory} that ramps up and back
        down, e.g.\ \texttt{TimeHistory([0, 0.4, 0.8], [0, 1000, 0])}, and
        measure the irrecoverable displacement.  The area enclosed by
        the loading--unloading loop is the energy dissipated by friction and by
        rearrangement, and is a far more discriminating validation target than
        the loading branch alone.
  \item \textbf{Restitution calibration.}  Extend Table~\ref{tab:restitution}
        into a calibration curve mapping the input $e$ onto the effective
        restitution, and use it to select the input required by a given
        material.
  \item \textbf{Wall friction and the Janssen effect.}  The side walls of the
        reference box carry \num{22}\% of the self-weight and more than half of
        the applied load.  Repeating the loading with smooth side walls
        (\texttt{Wall(friction=0.0)}) and with wider boxes would separate the
        response of the packing from that of the container.
  \item \textbf{Deposition under water.}  Rain the reference box dry and under
        water and compare the packings.  Drag slows the particles before
        impact, which should change the area fraction, the coordination number
        and the stiffness of the packing.
  \item \textbf{Submerged footing.}  Repeat the bearing test of
        Section~\ref{sec:footing} with the water table above the fill.  If the
        capacity scales with the unit weight of the fill, it should fall to
        about \num{60}\% of the dry value, the ratio of buoyant to dry weight.
  \item \textbf{Downward seepage and changing water tables.}  A downward flow
        ($U<0$) increases the effective stress and should compact the bed; a
        rising or falling water table changes it in the same way, but without
        flow.
  \item \textbf{Liquefaction.}  Apply a \texttt{Sinusoidal} load to a loose
        packing in coupled, viscous water.  If the skeleton tends to
        contract, the excess pore pressure should accumulate from cycle to cycle
        until the effective stress, measured on the floor, vanishes.  Comparing
        loose and dense packings would show the role of dilatancy.
  \item \textbf{Fouled ballast.}  Repeat the saturated ballast case of
        Section~\ref{sec:ballast} with coupled water whose viscosity is raised
        until the bed drains like fouled ballast.  The bulk modulus of the
        water must then be raised as well, towards its real value, so that it
        stays much stiffer than the ballast skeleton, whose constrained
        modulus is of order \qty{40}{\mega\pascal}; the stiff grains keep the
        time step small enough to afford it.  Whether excess pore pressure
        then accumulates from cycle to cycle is the question.
  \item \textbf{Wet friction and a soft formation.}  Two effects of water that
        the ballast study leaves out would need new inputs: a lower friction
        coefficient for contacts below the water table, and a formation that
        deforms instead of a rigid wall, e.g.\ a layer of fine, soft particles
        whose stiffness falls when it is saturated.
  \item \textbf{Two-way coupled flow.}  The coupled model solves the excess
        pressure but not the motion of the water itself.  Dilute, fast flows --
        a bed fluidized well beyond $U_{mf}$, a sediment plume -- would need the
        locally averaged Navier--Stokes equations solved on the grid instead.
\end{enumerate}
