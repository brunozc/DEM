\chapter{Mathematical formulation}
\label{cha:formulation}

This chapter states the complete continuous model that \texttt{DEMcookies}
discretises: the degrees of freedom and their mass properties, the Newton--Euler
equations of motion, the contact kinematics, the Hertz--Mindlin constitutive law
with viscous dissipation and Coulomb friction, and the boundary conditions
imposed by the walls and by the loading platen.

\section{Degrees of freedom and kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

The assembly consists of $N$ circular particles.  Particle $i$ is fully
described by its radius $R_{i}$ and by the state vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}_{i}
  = \bigl(x_{i},\, y_{i},\, \theta_{i}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}},
  \qquad i = 1,\dots,N,
  \label{eq:dofs}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ two translations of the centre $\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})$ and one
rotation $\theta_{i}$ about the out-of-plane axis $\mathbf{e}_{z}$.  The
corresponding velocities are $\mathbf{v}_{i}=\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}$ and
$\omega_{i}=\dot{\theta}_{i}$.  The rotation angle itself never enters the
constitutive law -- only $\omega_{i}$ does -- and is therefore not stored by
the code.

The radii are drawn from a uniform distribution,
\begin{equation}
  R_{i} \sim \mathcal{U}(R_{\min},R_{\max}),
  \label{eq:radii}
\end{equation}
so the assembly is polydisperse.  Polydispersity is essential: a monodisperse
two-dimensional assembly crystallises into a hexagonal lattice whose mechanical
response is strongly anisotropic and unrepresentative of a granular material.

\section{Mass properties}
\label{sec:massprops}

The mass and the polar moment of inertia used by the code are those of a
\emph{sphere} of radius $R_{i}$:
\begin{equation}
  m_{i} = \frac{4}{3}\pi\rho R_{i}^{3},
  \qquad
  I_{i} = \frac{2}{5} m_{i} R_{i}^{2}
        = \frac{8}{15}\pi\rho R_{i}^{5}.
  \label{eq:massprops}
\end{equation}
This is a deliberate modelling choice.  A genuine two-dimensional disc of unit
thickness would have $m_{i}=\pi\rho R_{i}^{2}$ and $I_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}m_{i}R_{i}^{2}$.
Using~\eqref{eq:massprops} together with the Hertzian sphere--sphere contact law
of Section~\ref{sec:normal} keeps the \emph{ratio} of inertia to contact
stiffness consistent with a three-dimensional grain, so that collision times,
restitution and the Rayleigh time step retain their physical meaning.  The
assembly should therefore be read as a planar arrangement of spheres, not as a
plane-strain slice through cylinders.  The practical consequence is that the
mass ratio between the smallest and the largest grain scales as
$(R_{\max}/R_{\min})^{3}$ rather than $(R_{\max}/R_{\min})^{2}$.

\section{Equations of motion}
\label{sec:eom}

Let $\mathcal{C}_{i}$ be the set of particles in contact with $i$ and
$\mathcal{W}_{i}$ the set of boundaries (walls and platen) in contact with $i$.
The Newton--Euler equations are
\begin{align}
  m_{i}\,\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
    &= \sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}} \mathbf{F}_{ij}
     + \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{i}} \mathbf{F}_{iw}
     + m_{i}\,\mathbf{g},
     \label{eq:newton}\\[2pt]
  I_{i}\,\dot{\omega}_{i}
    &= \sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}} T_{ij}
     + \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{i}} T_{iw},
     \label{eq:euler}
\end{align}
with the body force $\mathbf{g}=-g\,\mathbf{e}_{y}$.  No damping other than the
contact dissipation of Sections~\ref{sec:normal-damping}
and~\ref{sec:tangential} is applied: a particle in flight is a free projectile.

Newton's third law is enforced pairwise,
$\mathbf{F}_{ji}=-\mathbf{F}_{ij}$, so that linear momentum is conserved exactly
by the force evaluation; angular momentum is likewise conserved because the
torques are computed from the same tangential force acting at the same contact
point on both bodies (Section~\ref{sec:torque}).

\section{Contact kinematics}
\label{sec:contact-kinematics}

\subsection{Overlap and local frame}

Two particles $i$ and $j$ are in contact when their circles intersect.  With
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{d}_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j},
  \qquad
  d_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf{d}_{ij}\rVert,
\end{equation}
the \emph{normal overlap} (or interpenetration) is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\delta_{n} = R_{i}+R_{j}-d_{ij}\;}
  \qquad\text{contact}\iff \delta_{n}>0 .
  \label{eq:overlap}
\end{equation}
The local orthonormal frame is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{d}_{ij}}{d_{ij}} = (n_{x},n_{y}),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{e}_{z}\times\mathbf{n} = (-n_{y},\,n_{x}),
  \label{eq:frame}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{n}$ points from $j$ towards $i$, i.e.\ in the direction in which
$i$ is pushed away.  In the soft-sphere paradigm $\delta_{n}$ is not a geometric
artefact but the physical approach of the two centres caused by the elastic
flattening of the contact region.

The lever arm from the centre of $i$ to the contact point is taken as
$-R_{i}\mathbf{n}$ (and $+R_{j}\mathbf{n}$ for $j$), i.e.\ the undeformed
surface point.  The error introduced by neglecting the $\delta_{n}/2$ correction
is of the same order as the overlap itself and is negligible whenever the model
is used within its range of validity.

\subsection{Relative surface velocity}

The velocity of the material point of $i$ at the contact is
$\mathbf{v}_{i}+\omega_{i}\mathbf{e}_{z}\times(-R_{i}\mathbf{n})
 = \mathbf{v}_{i}-R_{i}\omega_{i}\mathbf{t}$, and similarly for $j$.  The
relative surface velocity of $i$ with respect to $j$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \mathbf{v}_{ij}
  = (\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{j})
    - \bigl(R_{i}\omega_{i}+R_{j}\omega_{j}\bigr)\,\mathbf{t}\;}
  \label{eq:vrel}
\end{equation}
Note the \emph{sum} of the two spin contributions: both particles rotating in
the same sense produce sliding of the same sign at the contact.  Decomposing
into the local frame,
\begin{equation}
  v_{n} = \mathbf{v}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{n},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{v}_{t} = \mathbf{v}_{ij} - v_{n}\mathbf{n},
  \label{eq:vn-vt}
\end{equation}
with the sign convention $v_{n}<0$ during approach (loading) and $v_{n}>0$
during separation (unloading).

\subsection{Effective contact quantities}

Two bodies in contact are replaced by an equivalent sphere--plane problem
through the effective radius and effective mass,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{R^{*}} = \frac{1}{R_{i}} + \frac{1}{R_{j}}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  R^{*}=\frac{R_{i}R_{j}}{R_{i}+R_{j}},
  \qquad
  \frac{1}{m^{*}} = \frac{1}{m_{i}} + \frac{1}{m_{j}}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  m^{*}=\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{m_{i}+m_{j}} .
  \label{eq:effective}
\end{equation}
The effective elastic moduli follow from the Hertz and Mindlin
solutions~\cite{johnson1985},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{E^{*}} = \frac{1-\nu_{i}^{2}}{E_{i}} + \frac{1-\nu_{j}^{2}}{E_{j}},
  \qquad
  \frac{1}{G^{*}} = \frac{2-\nu_{i}}{G_{i}} + \frac{2-\nu_{j}}{G_{j}} ,
  \label{eq:moduli-general}
\end{equation}
which, for the single material assumed here ($E_{i}=E_{j}=E$,
$\nu_{i}=\nu_{j}=\nu$), reduce to the constants evaluated once at
construction time:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  E^{*} = \frac{E}{2\,(1-\nu^{2})},
  \qquad
  G^{*} = \frac{G}{2\,(2-\nu)},
  \qquad
  G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} \;}
  \label{eq:moduli}
\end{equation}

\section{Normal contact: the Hertz law}
\label{sec:normal}

\subsection{Elastic force}

For two frictionless elastic spheres pressed together by an approach
$\delta_{n}$, Hertz's solution~\cite{hertz1882,johnson1985} gives a circular
contact patch of radius
\begin{equation}
  a = \sqrt{R^{*}\delta_{n}},
  \label{eq:contact-radius}
\end{equation}
a semi-elliptical pressure distribution
$p(r)=p_{0}\sqrt{1-r^{2}/a^{2}}$ with $p_{0}=3F_{n}/(2\pi a^{2})$, and the
non-linear force--displacement relation
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  F_{n}^{\mathrm{e}}
  = \frac{4}{3}\,E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}}\;\delta_{n}^{3/2}
  = k_{n}\,\delta_{n}^{3/2},
  \qquad
  k_{n} \equiv \frac{4}{3}E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}} \;}
  \label{eq:hertz}
\end{equation}
The stiffening exponent $3/2$ reflects the growth of the contact area with
load; it is the single most important qualitative difference with respect to
linear (Cundall--Strack) spring models.  Differentiating~\eqref{eq:hertz} gives
the \emph{tangent} normal stiffness, which is the quantity that controls both the
damping coefficient and the numerical stability:
\begin{equation}
  S_{n} = \frac{\partial F_{n}^{\mathrm{e}}}{\partial \delta_{n}}
        = 2\,E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}\delta_{n}}
        = 2\,E^{*}a .
  \label{eq:Sn}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Viscous dissipation}
\label{sec:normal-damping}

Real collisions dissipate energy.  Following Tsuji \emph{et
al.}~\cite{tsuji1992}, a dashpot whose coefficient scales with the instantaneous
Hertzian stiffness is added in parallel with the spring,
\begin{equation}
  F_{n}^{\mathrm{d}} = -\,c_{n}\,v_{n},
  \qquad
  \boxed{\;c_{n} = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{6}}\;\beta\;\sqrt{S_{n}\,m^{*}}\;}
  \label{eq:cn}
\end{equation}
where $2\sqrt{S_{n}m^{*}}$ is the critical damping of the equivalent
single-degree-of-freedom oscillator and $\sqrt{5/6}\,\beta$ is the damping
ratio.  The dimensionless factor $\beta$ is obtained by matching the
coefficient of restitution $e$ of the equivalent linear oscillator,
\begin{equation}
  e = \exp\!\left(\frac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^{2}}}\right)
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \boxed{\;
  \beta = \frac{\lvert \ln e\rvert}{\sqrt{\ln^{2}e+\pi^{2}}}\;}
  \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Limiting cases: $e\to1$ gives $\beta\to0$ (elastic, non-dissipative contact),
while $e\to0$ gives $\beta\to1$ (fully plastic collision).  The implementation
clips $e$ to $[10^{-4},1]$ so that~\eqref{eq:beta} is always well defined.

The total normal force is the sum of the two contributions, truncated at zero
because the model is cohesionless:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  F_{n} = \max\!\Bigl(0,\;
          k_{n}\delta_{n}^{3/2} - c_{n} v_{n}\Bigr) \;}
  \label{eq:Fn}
\end{equation}
The truncation is physically necessary: at the very end of a collision the
elastic term vanishes faster than the damping term, and without the cut-off the
dashpot would pull the separating particles back together (spurious attraction)
and could even prevent detachment~\cite{direnzo2004}.

\section{Tangential contact: Mindlin spring with Coulomb limit}
\label{sec:tangential}

\subsection{Tangential stiffness}

The Mindlin no-slip solution~\cite{mindlin1949,mindlin1953} for two spheres
pressed together and then sheared gives the tangential stiffness
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;S_{t} = 8\,G^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}\delta_{n}} = 8\,G^{*}a\;}
  \label{eq:St}
\end{equation}
Like $S_{n}$, it depends on the current overlap: a contact that is more strongly
compressed is also stiffer in shear.  Their ratio is a constant of the material,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{S_{t}}{S_{n}} = \frac{4G^{*}}{E^{*}}
  = \frac{4\,\dfrac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\dfrac{1}{2(2-\nu)}}
         {\dfrac{E}{2(1-\nu^{2})}}
  = \frac{2(1-\nu)}{2-\nu} ,
\end{equation}
which equals $0.857$ for $\nu=0.25$.

\subsection{Tangential history}

Friction is path dependent, so the tangential force cannot be a function of the
instantaneous state alone.  A tangential spring $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ -- the
accumulated relative tangential displacement since the contact was created -- is
stored for every contact and updated incrementally.  Two operations are applied
in sequence at each evaluation.

\paragraph{(i) Rotation of the contact plane.}  Between two evaluations the
normal $\mathbf{n}$ changes direction, so the stored spring may acquire a
component along the new normal.  It is removed by projection onto the current
tangent plane,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\xi} \;\leftarrow\;
  \boldsymbol{\xi} - (\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{n} .
  \label{eq:xi-project}
\end{equation}
This is the standard first-order treatment~\cite{silbert2001}; it keeps the
spring admissible without rotating its magnitude, and the associated error is
$O(\Delta t^{2})$ per step.

\paragraph{(ii) Incremental accumulation.}  The spring is then extended by the
tangential relative displacement accumulated over the step,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\xi} \;\leftarrow\;
  \boldsymbol{\xi} + \mathbf{v}_{t}\,\Delta t .
  \label{eq:xi-accumulate}
\end{equation}
A contact that closes for the first time starts from
$\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{0}$; a contact that opens is deleted together with its
history, so that re-closing later starts again from zero.

\subsection{Trial force and Coulomb return mapping}

The trial tangential force combines the Mindlin spring with a dashpot built on
the tangential stiffness in exactly the same way as~\eqref{eq:cn},
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}
  = -\,S_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\xi} - c_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{t},
  \qquad
  c_{t} = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{6}}\;\beta\;\sqrt{S_{t}\,m^{*}} .
  \label{eq:Ft-trial}
\end{equation}
Sliding is then enforced through the Coulomb criterion.  If the trial force
violates the friction cone it is scaled back onto it, and -- crucially -- the
stored spring is reset so that it remains consistent with the force actually
transmitted:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{if } \lVert \mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rVert \le \mu F_{n}:
      &&\mathbf{F}_{t} = \mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}
        &&\text{(stick)}\\[2pt]
    &\text{if } \lVert \mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rVert > \mu F_{n}:
      &&\mathbf{F}_{t}
        = \mu F_{n}\,
          \frac{\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}}
               {\lVert \mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rVert},
      \quad
      \boldsymbol{\xi} = -\frac{\mathbf{F}_{t}}{S_{t}}
        &&\text{(slip)}
  \end{aligned}\;}
  \label{eq:coulomb}
\end{equation}
The structure is identical to the radial return mapping of computational
plasticity: the spring plays the role of the elastic strain, the friction cone
of the yield surface, and the reset of $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ of the plastic
corrector.  Without the reset the spring would keep growing during prolonged
sliding and would produce a spurious force spike on reversal.

Two properties of~\eqref{eq:coulomb} are worth noting.  First, because the reset
uses only the spring term, the damping contribution is discarded during sliding
and the transmitted force is exactly $\mu F_{n}$.  Second, since $F_{n}=0$
implies $\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{0}$, a contact that is closing with negligible
compression transmits no shear.

\section{Contact force and torque}
\label{sec:torque}

The total force exerted by $j$ on $i$ and its reaction are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{ij} = F_{n}\,\mathbf{n} + \mathbf{F}_{t},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{F}_{ji} = -\mathbf{F}_{ij}.
  \label{eq:Fij}
\end{equation}
Only the tangential part produces a moment about the particle centres.  With the
lever arms $-R_{i}\mathbf{n}$ and $+R_{j}\mathbf{n}$,
\begin{align}
  T_{ij} &= \bigl[(-R_{i}\mathbf{n})\times\mathbf{F}_{t}\bigr]_{z}
          = -R_{i}\,\bigl(n_{x}F_{ty}-n_{y}F_{tx}\bigr),
          \label{eq:Ti}\\
  T_{ji} &= \bigl[(+R_{j}\mathbf{n})\times(-\mathbf{F}_{t})\bigr]_{z}
          = -R_{j}\,\bigl(n_{x}F_{ty}-n_{y}F_{tx}\bigr).
          \label{eq:Tj}
\end{align}
Both torques carry the same sign, which is the expected behaviour: friction at
a contact spins the two grains in the same sense, like two meshing gears rolling
on each other.  Because $\mathbf{F}_{t}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{t}$, the common
factor simplifies to the signed tangential force,
\begin{equation}
  n_{x}F_{ty}-n_{y}F_{tx} = \mathbf{F}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{t}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  T_{ij} = -R_{i}\,(\mathbf{F}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{t}),
  \quad
  T_{ji} = -R_{j}\,(\mathbf{F}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{t}).
\end{equation}
Without rolling resistance ($\mu_{r}=0$, the default) these are the only
torques, so a lone particle on a flat wall keeps spinning indefinitely once it
has been set into rotation.

\section{Rolling resistance}
\label{sec:rolling}

Spheres and discs roll past each other far too easily.  Angular grains cannot:
a grain resting on a flat facet must lift its centre over the edge of the facet
before it can roll, so a packing of angular grains such as railway ballast is
much stronger than one of round particles with the same friction.  The standard
surrogate for that shape effect is a moment that resists the relative rotation
of two bodies in contact~\cite{iwashita1998}.  DEMcookies uses the
elastic--plastic spring--dashpot model, type~C in the classification of Ai et
al.~\cite{ai2011}.

\paragraph{Rolling history.}  The relative rotation of the two bodies is
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{r} = \omega_{i} - \omega_{j},
  \qquad
  \theta_{r} \;\leftarrow\; \theta_{r} + \omega_{r}\,\Delta t ,
  \label{eq:theta-r}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_{j}=0$ for a wall or platen, which never rotates.  Like the
tangential spring, $\theta_{r}$ starts from zero when a contact closes and is
deleted when it opens.  A rigid rotation of the two bodies together leaves
$\omega_{r}=0$, so the measure is objective.

\paragraph{Moment.}  The trial moment on body $i$ combines a spring and a
dashpot,
\begin{equation}
  M_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}} = -k_{r}\,\theta_{r} - c_{r}\,\omega_{r},
  \qquad
  k_{r} = 2.25\,\mu_{r}^{2}\,R^{*2}\,S_{n},
  \qquad
  c_{r} = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{6}}\;\beta\;\sqrt{k_{r}\,I_{r}} ,
  \label{eq:Mr-trial}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{r}$ is the rolling friction coefficient of the material and $S_{n}$
the tangent normal stiffness~\eqref{eq:Sn}.  The stiffness is that of Ai et
al., written with the Hertzian $S_{n}$ in place of a linear normal spring.  The
damping follows the normal and tangential dashpots, built on the effective
moment of inertia about the contact point, about which a grain rolls:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{I_{r}} = \frac{1}{I_{i}+m_{i}R_{i}^{2}} + \frac{1}{I_{j}+m_{j}R_{j}^{2}},
  \qquad
  I_{r} = I_{i}+m_{i}R_{i}^{2} = 1.4\,m_{i}R_{i}^{2}\ \text{against a wall}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Plastic limit.}  The moment is capped, and the spring reset, exactly
as the tangential force is by~\eqref{eq:coulomb}:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \lvert M_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rvert \le \mu_{r} R^{*} F_{n}:
    \ M_{r} = M_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}};
  \qquad
  \text{otherwise}\ \
  M_{r} = \mu_{r} R^{*} F_{n}\,\operatorname{sign}M_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}},
  \quad \theta_{r} = -\frac{M_{r}}{k_{r}} .\;}
  \label{eq:rolling-limit}
\end{equation}
The limit has a simple meaning: $\mu_{r}R^{*}$ is the largest lever arm of the
normal force about the centre, as if the grain stood on a flat facet of
half-width $\mu_{r}R^{*}$.  Body $i$ receives $M_{r}$ and body $j$ the opposite
moment, $-M_{r}$; no force is involved.

\paragraph{Closed-form check.}  A sphere on an incline of angle $\alpha$, with
enough friction not to slide, is held by the rolling moment as long as the
moment of its weight about the contact point, $mgR\sin\alpha$, stays below
$\mu_{r}R\,mg\cos\alpha$, i.e.\ while $\tan\alpha<\mu_{r}$.  On a steeper
incline it rolls with the constant acceleration
\begin{equation}
  a = \frac{g\,(\sin\alpha - \mu_{r}\cos\alpha)}{1 + I/(mR^{2})}
    = \frac{g\,(\sin\alpha - \mu_{r}\cos\alpha)}{1.4} ,
  \label{eq:incline}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the textbook $g\sin\alpha/1.4$ without rolling resistance.
The test suite checks both: a \qty{10}{\milli\metre} sphere with $\mu_{r}=0.4$
on a gradient of $0.32$ moves only by the \qty{40}{\micro\metre} of elastic
deformation of its springs, while on a gradient of $0.48$ the simulated
accelerations for $\mu_{r}=0$, $0.2$ and $0.4$ match~\eqref{eq:incline} to four
significant digits (\qtylist{3.0322;1.7688;0.5054}{\metre\per\second\squared}).

\paragraph{Choice of $\mu_{r}$.}  Values of $0.05$ to $0.5$ cover round to very
angular grains~\cite{ai2011}.  The rolling mode does not shorten the stable time
step: its angular frequency $\sqrt{k_{r}/I_{r}}$ is at most $1.27\,\mu_{r}$
times the normal one, so below it for $\mu_{r}<0.79$.

\section{Summary of the contact law}
\label{sec:contact-summary}

Given $(\mathbf{n},\delta_{n},\mathbf{v}_{ij},\boldsymbol{\xi},\Delta t,
R^{*},m^{*})$, one evaluation of the constitutive law proceeds as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.4em,label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item If $\delta_{n}\le0$: return zero force and $\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{0}$.
  \item Contact radius and stiffnesses:
        $a=\sqrt{R^{*}\delta_{n}}$, $S_{n}=2E^{*}a$, $S_{t}=8G^{*}a$,
        $k_{n}=\tfrac{4}{3}E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}}$.
  \item Normal: $v_{n}=\mathbf{v}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{n}$,
        $c_{n}=2\sqrt{5/6}\,\beta\sqrt{S_{n}m^{*}}$,
        $F_{n}=\max(0,\ k_{n}\delta_{n}^{3/2}-c_{n}v_{n})$.
  \item Tangential kinematics: $\mathbf{v}_{t}=\mathbf{v}_{ij}-v_{n}\mathbf{n}$.
  \item History: project $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ with~\eqref{eq:xi-project}, then
        accumulate with~\eqref{eq:xi-accumulate}.
  \item Trial force~\eqref{eq:Ft-trial} and Coulomb
        correction~\eqref{eq:coulomb}.
  \item Assemble $\mathbf{F}=F_{n}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{F}_{t}$ and return
        $\mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{F}_{t}$ and the updated $\boldsymbol{\xi}$.
  \item If $\mu_{r}>0$: accumulate $\theta_{r}$ with~\eqref{eq:theta-r}, form
        the trial moment~\eqref{eq:Mr-trial}, cap it
        with~\eqref{eq:rolling-limit} and return $M_{r}$ and the updated
        $\theta_{r}$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:walls}

\subsection{Polygonal domain}
\label{sec:domain}

The domain is a simple polygon with vertices
$\mathbf{p}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{M-1}$.  Edge $k$ is the segment from
$\mathbf{a}_{k}=\mathbf{p}_{k}$ to $\mathbf{b}_{k}=\mathbf{p}_{k+1}$, the last
edge closing the polygon back to $\mathbf{p}_{0}$, and $\mathbf{n}_{k}$ is its
unit normal pointing into the domain.  The vertices may be given in either
orientation; the sign of the polygon area decides on which side of each edge the
inward normal lies.  Every edge carries one of three boundary conditions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[Wall] a rigid, fixed, frictional wall.  Its friction coefficient
        $\mu_{w}$ defaults to the material coefficient $\mu$ and may be set per
        wall; $\mu_{w}=0$ gives a smooth wall, e.g.\ a symmetry plane.
  \item[Open] no wall.  Particles may cross the edge; a particle whose centre
        has left the polygon by more than an escape margin is removed from the
        simulation (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).
  \item[Loaded platen] a rigid body that applies a prescribed load $F(t)$
        (Section~\ref{sec:platen}).  Edges that carry the same platen form one
        body.  It acts as a fixed wall until loading starts.
\end{description}
The container of Figure~\ref{fig:problem} is the special case $M=4$ with walls
on the bottom and the sides and a loaded platen on top.  The location at which a
load is applied is defined through the geometry: to load only part of a side,
for instance with a strip footing, that side is split into collinear edges and
only the middle one is made a loaded platen.  A load that does not act on a
straight line, such as that of a wedge or a curved indenter, is applied through
a body made of several edges with different orientations.

\subsection{Particle--edge contact}

A solid edge (wall or platen) is treated as a body of infinite radius and
infinite mass, so that~\eqref{eq:effective} degenerates to
\begin{equation}
  R^{*} = \lim_{R_{j}\to\infty} \frac{R_{i}R_{j}}{R_{i}+R_{j}} = R_{i},
  \qquad
  m^{*} = \lim_{m_{j}\to\infty} \frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{m_{i}+m_{j}} = m_{i}.
  \label{eq:wall-effective}
\end{equation}
The contact point $\mathbf{c}$ is the point of the edge closest to the particle
centre,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} + \tau\,(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}),
  \qquad
  \tau = \min\!\left(1,\ \max\!\left(0,\
         \frac{(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})}
              {\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\rVert^{2}}\right)\right),
  \label{eq:closest-point}
\end{equation}
and the overlap and normal follow from the distance to it,
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{n} = R_{i} - \lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{c}\rVert,
  \qquad
  \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{c}}
                    {\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{c}\rVert}.
  \label{eq:edge-overlap}
\end{equation}
For $0<\tau<1$ the normal is the edge normal and $\delta_{n}$ is the penetration
of the particle into the edge; for $\tau\in\{0,1\}$ the particle touches an end
vertex and the normal points from the vertex to the centre.  A centre lying
exactly on the edge, where~\eqref{eq:edge-overlap} is undefined, is pushed along
the inward normal $\mathbf{n}_{k}$.  Table~\ref{tab:boundaries} lists the
resulting overlaps for the rectangular container.

\paragraph{Shared vertices.}  A vertex shared by two solid edges belongs to
both.  When the closest point of one of them to a particle is the shared vertex,
the other edge either reports the same vertex or has a closer point of its own;
in both cases the contact is already represented by the other edge, and the
vertex contact is discarded.  Without this rule a particle resting on a
re-entrant corner, or on a floor built from several collinear edges, would feel
two contacts and settle at the overlap of half its weight.  At a convex corner
of the domain (interior angle below \ang{180}) a particle inside the domain
never has a vertex as its closest point, so the rule matters only at re-entrant
corners and between collinear edges.

Because a fixed wall neither translates nor spins, the relative surface velocity
\eqref{eq:vrel} loses the $j$ terms,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{iw} = \mathbf{v}_{i} - R_{i}\omega_{i}\,\mathbf{t} .
  \label{eq:vrel-wall}
\end{equation}
Apart from these substitutions the constitutive law of
Section~\ref{sec:contact-summary} is applied unchanged, including the tangential
history and the per-edge friction coefficient: walls are frictional and can
therefore support shear, which is what produces the Janssen-like arching
observed in tall containers.  Edges are segments, so a particle that has left
the domain can still touch a wall from the outside.

\subsection{Loaded bodies}
\label{sec:platen}

A loaded body, or platen, is a rigid body of mass $m_{p}$ formed by a set
$\mathcal{K}_{p}$ of edges: every edge that carries the same
\texttt{LoadedPlaten} object belongs to it.  It can therefore be a single plate,
a wedge, or a polygonal approximation of a curved surface.  The body has a
single degree of freedom, the translation $s_{p}$ along a unit direction
$\mathbf{d}_{p}$, so the end points of its edges are
$\mathbf{a}_{k}+s_{p}\mathbf{d}_{p}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{k}+s_{p}\mathbf{d}_{p}$,
$k\in\mathcal{K}_{p}$, and its state is $(s_{p},v_{p})$ with $v_{p}=\dot s_{p}$.
The direction can be given explicitly, e.g.\ to push a curved or inclined body
vertically; otherwise it is the length-weighted mean of the inward normals of
the edges,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{d}_{p} = \frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{p}} L_{k}\,\mathbf{n}_{k}}
                        {\bigl\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{p}} L_{k}\,\mathbf{n}_{k}\bigr\rVert},
  \label{eq:platen-direction}
\end{equation}
with $L_{k}$ the length of edge $k$.  For a single edge this is its inward
normal; a body whose normals cancel out, such as the top and bottom of a box,
needs an explicit direction.  Because the edges of a body move together, the
vertices they share stay shared, and the vertex rule of
Section~\ref{sec:walls} still applies: the tip of a wedge touches a particle
through one contact, not two.

Unlike the fixed walls the body moves, so its velocity enters the relative
surface velocity of every particle it touches,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{ip} = \bigl(\mathbf{v}_{i}-v_{p}\mathbf{d}_{p}\bigr)
                    - R_{i}\omega_{i}\,\mathbf{t}.
  \label{eq:vrel-platen}
\end{equation}
Its effective radius and mass are still those of~\eqref{eq:wall-effective}: the
body is rigid and its finite mass appears only in its own equation of motion,
not in the contact damping.

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of particles touching any edge of the body and
$\mathbf{F}_{ip}$ the force the body exerts on particle $i$.  By Newton's third
law the particles push the body back with
\begin{equation}
  F_{p} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}} \mathbf{F}_{ip}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{p},
  \label{eq:platen-reaction}
\end{equation}
which is positive when the packing resists the load.  The equation of motion of
the body reads
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  m_{p}\,\dot v_{p} = F(t) - F_{p} + \chi\, m_{p}\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{p},
  \qquad
  \dot s_{p} = v_{p} \;}
  \label{eq:platen-eom}
\end{equation}
where $F(t)$ is the prescribed load, positive when it pushes the body along
$\mathbf{d}_{p}$, and $\chi\in\{0,1\}$ switches the self-weight of the body on.
By default $\chi=0$, so the mass acts purely as an inertial regulariser.
Equation~\eqref{eq:platen-eom} makes the loading a \emph{force-controlled}
boundary condition: the body finds its own displacement as the packing resists.
In equilibrium $F_{p}=F(t)$, and the mass controls only how quickly that balance
is reached; a heavier body filters the high-frequency contact noise at the price
of a slower approach to equilibrium.  The edges of a body are segments, so
particles that flow around its ends and settle behind it push it along
$\mathbf{d}_{p}$ and reduce $F_{p}$, as they would on a thin buried plate or
shell.

\paragraph{Seating.}  So that loading starts without an impact, the body is
first translated along $\mathbf{d}_{p}$ until one of its edges touches the first
particle in its way.  Seen from an edge, the particle moves along
$\mathbf{u}=-\mathbf{d}_{p}$, and the travel $s$ at which edge $k$ first touches
particle $i$ is the smaller of two candidates.  The interior of the edge is
touched when the distance of the centre from the line of the edge falls to the
radius,
\begin{equation}
  s_{\text{line}} = \frac{\lvert h_{i}\rvert - R_{i}}
                         {\lvert\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}\rvert},
  \qquad h_{i} = (\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k},
  \label{eq:seat-line}
\end{equation}
provided the particle approaches the line ($h_{i}\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}<0$)
and the foot of the centre then lies on the edge; $\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}$ is a unit
normal of the edge.  An end point $\mathbf{p}$ of the edge is touched at the
first root of $\lVert\mathbf{w}+s\mathbf{u}\rVert=R_{i}$,
$\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{p}$,
\begin{equation}
  s_{\text{end}} = -\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{u}
     - \sqrt{(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{u})^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}+R_{i}^{2}} .
  \label{eq:seat-end}
\end{equation}
The seating translation $s_{p}^{0}$ is the smallest non-negative candidate over
all edges of the body and all particles.  The body is never moved backwards: if
a particle ahead of it already overlaps one of its edges, it stays where it is.
Seating can be switched off per body.  The recorded displacement is measured
from the seated position,
\begin{equation}
  u_{p}(t) = s_{p}(t) - s_{p}^{0}.
  \label{eq:platen-disp}
\end{equation}
For the top platen of Figure~\ref{fig:problem}, $\mathbf{d}_{p}=(0,-1)$, so
$s_{p}$ is the downward translation, \eqref{eq:seat-line} reduces to the gap
between the plate and the highest particle below it, and
\eqref{eq:platen-reaction}, \eqref{eq:platen-eom} reduce to the vertical force
balance of a horizontal plate.  A domain may contain several bodies; they all
start to move when loading starts and each follows its own load history.

\subsection{Prescribed load history}

The applied load is a function $F(t)$ of the load clock $t$, which is reset when
the platens are released and therefore measures time since the start of loading,
not since the start of the simulation.  Four histories are provided; any other
function of time may be supplied directly.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[Pulse] a rectangular pulse of amplitude $A$ and duration $T$ starting at
        $t_{0}$: $F=A$ for $t_{0}\le t<t_{0}+T$ and $F=0$ otherwise.
  \item[Heaviside] a step of amplitude $A$ at $t_{0}$, optionally with a linear
        rise of duration $t_{r}$,
        \begin{equation}
          F(t) =
          \begin{cases}
            0, & t < t_{0},\\[2pt]
            A\,\dfrac{t-t_{0}}{t_{r}}, & t_{0}\le t<t_{0}+t_{r},\\[6pt]
            A, & t\ge t_{0}+t_{r}.
          \end{cases}
          \label{eq:ramp}
        \end{equation}
        With $t_{r}=0$ it is a sudden step; the reference simulation uses
        $A=F_{\max}$, $t_{0}=0$ and $t_{r}>0$, a linear ramp followed by a
        hold.
  \item[Sinusoidal] a harmonic history
        $F(t)=F_{m}+A\sin\bigl(2\pi f\,(t-t_{0})+\varphi\bigr)$ for $t\ge t_{0}$
        and zero before.  A platen cannot pull on cohesionless particles, so
        cyclic compression needs $F_{m}\ge A$; $F_{m}=A$ with
        $\varphi=-\pi/2$ starts smoothly from zero.
  \item[Time history] a user-defined table $(t_{k},F_{k})$ with strictly
        increasing $t_{k}$, interpolated linearly and held constant before the
        first and after the last point.  Unloading and reloading branches are
        modelled this way.
\end{description}
The rise time, or the time scale of any other history, must be long compared
with the characteristic contact time~\eqref{eq:tc}, otherwise the response is
dynamic rather than quasi-static.

\section{Pore water}
\label{sec:water}

Optionally, the particles can be immersed in water below a horizontal free
surface, the water table, at height $y_{w}(t)$.  Two levels of coupling between
the water and the particles are available.  With \emph{drained} water the pore
pressure stays hydrostatic and any flow through the packing is prescribed; the
water acts on the particles through buoyancy and the force of the water flowing
past them.  With \emph{coupled} water an excess pore pressure is solved in
addition (Section~\ref{sec:coupled-water}): it builds up when the skeleton
changes volume faster than the water can flow in or out, and dissipates by flow,
so undrained loading and consolidation can be modelled.  In both cases the
forces of the water are added to the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:newton},
\begin{equation}
  m_{i}\,\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
    = \sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}} \mathbf{F}_{ij}
     + \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{i}} \mathbf{F}_{iw}
     + m_{i}\,\mathbf{g}
     + \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,b} + \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,f} + \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,p},
  \label{eq:newton-water}
\end{equation}
with the buoyancy $\mathbf{F}^{\,b}$, the flow force $\mathbf{F}^{\,f}$ and, for
coupled water, the excess pore pressure force $\mathbf{F}^{\,p}$.  The contact
forces in~\eqref{eq:newton-water} are then the intergranular forces that make up
the \emph{effective} stress: a submerged packing at rest presses on its floor
with its buoyant weight, not with its weight.

\subsection{Buoyancy}

A sphere whose lowest point lies a depth $h$ below the free surface displaces
the volume of a spherical cap,
\begin{equation}
  V_{i}^{\,w} = \frac{\pi h^{2}\,(3R_{i}-h)}{3},
  \qquad h = \min\bigl(\max(y_{w}-y_{i}+R_{i},\,0),\ 2R_{i}\bigr),
  \label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
and the hydrostatic pressure pushes it up with
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,b} = \rho_{w}\,g\,V_{i}^{\,w}\,\mathbf{e}_{y}.
  \label{eq:buoyancy}
\end{equation}
Because~\eqref{eq:cap} is continuous, a particle crossing the free surface feels
no jump in force, and a particle lighter than water floats at the depth where
$V_{i}^{\,w}=m_{i}/\rho_{w}$.

\subsection{Flow past the particles}

The drag of water moving at the interstitial velocity $\mathbf{u}$ past a sphere
surrounded by others is given by the correlation of Di
Felice~\cite{difelice1994}, which extends the drag on an isolated sphere through
the local porosity $\varepsilon$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,d} =
     \tfrac{1}{2}\,C_{D}\,\rho_{w}\,\pi R_{i}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2-\chi}\,
     \lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert\,(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{i}),
  \label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
with the drag coefficient of an isolated sphere~\cite{dallavalle1948}, the
particle Reynolds number and the crowding exponent
\begin{equation}
  C_{D} = \Bigl(0.63 + \frac{4.8}{\sqrt{Re}}\Bigr)^{2},
  \qquad
  Re = \frac{\rho_{w}\,2R_{i}\,\varepsilon\,\lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert}{\mu_{w}},
  \qquad
  \chi = 3.7 - 0.65\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1.5-\log_{10}Re\bigr)^{2}\Bigr),
  \label{eq:drag-cd}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{w}$ is the dynamic viscosity.  For an isolated sphere
($\varepsilon=1$) at vanishing Reynolds number,~\eqref{eq:drag} tends to
$0.96\cdot 6\pi\mu_{w}R_{i}\lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert$, \num{96}\% of
Stokes' law; at large Reynolds number $C_{D}\to0.40$.  In a dense packing the
factor $\varepsilon^{2-\chi}$, with $\chi$ between \num{3.05} and \num{3.7}, raises
the drag several times, which reproduces hindered settling and the permeability
of a packed bed.

The drag is not the whole force of a flow through a packing.  To pass through
the pores the water needs a pressure gradient, and that gradient also pushes on
the volume of the particles.  For a uniform flow the momentum balance of the
water, which has negligible inertia, reads $-\varepsilon\nabla p = n\,
\mathbf{F}^{\,d}$ per unit volume, with $n=(1-\varepsilon)/V_{i}$ particles per
unit volume; the pressure gradient then pushes on each particle with
$-V_{i}\nabla p = \frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{F}^{\,d}$, and the two
together are~\cite{zhou2010}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,f} = \frac{V_{i}^{\,w}}{V_{i}}\;\frac{\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,d}}{\varepsilon}.
  \label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}
The factor $V_{i}^{\,w}/V_{i}$ scales the force on a particle cut by the free
surface.  For an isolated particle $\varepsilon\to1$ and~\eqref{eq:interaction}
reduces to the drag; in a dense packing ($\varepsilon\approx0.4$) it is more than
twice the drag.

\subsection{Porosity of a planar packing}

The drag correlation was established for packings of spheres, while the
porosity that can be measured in the model is that of a planar layer of discs,
$\varepsilon_{2D}$, which is much lower for the same arrangement (a dense
two-dimensional packing has $\varepsilon_{2D}\approx0.17$).  The two are related
by~\cite{hoomans1996}
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = 1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt{\sqrt{3}\,\pi}}\,\bigl(1-\varepsilon_{2D}\bigr)^{3/2},
  \label{eq:porosity3d}
\end{equation}
which maps the hexagonal close packing of discs onto the face-centred cubic
packing of spheres ($\varepsilon=0.2595$) and an empty layer onto pure water.
The local $\varepsilon_{2D}$ is averaged over cells of about two particle
diameters (Section~\ref{sec:water-numerics}).

\subsection{Seepage}

A vertical flow can be imposed through the packing, given by its superficial
(Darcy) velocity $U(t)$, positive upwards.  The flux is carried by the pore
space, so the interstitial velocity is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u} = \frac{U(t)}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{e}_{y}.
  \label{eq:interstitial}
\end{equation}
The flow is uniform, as in a permeameter whose floor and top let the water
through; it is not deflected by walls or particles.  An upward flow reduces the
effective weight of the packing.  It carries the packing entirely once its force
on the fixed bed equals the buoyant weight,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i}\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,f}(U_{mf})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{y}
  = \sum_{i}\bigl(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w}\bigr)\,V_{i}\,g ,
  \label{eq:umf}
\end{equation}
which defines the minimum fluidization velocity $U_{mf}$.  In soil mechanics this
is the quick condition, reached when the hydraulic gradient across the bed
equals the critical gradient $i_{c}=(\rho_{s}/\rho_{w}-1)(1-\varepsilon)$.  The
water level $y_{w}$ and the superficial velocity $U$ may both vary in time, to
model a rising water table, a drawdown or a gradually increasing flow.

\subsection{Coupled pore pressure}
\label{sec:coupled-water}

With coupled water the excess pore pressure $p$, above hydrostatic, is solved on
the porosity grid.  In a dense packing the water in the pores moves with the
skeleton, at the local mean solid velocity $\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{s}$, except for the
flux $\mathbf{q}$ relative to the solid that Darcy's law drives through the
pores.  Mass conservation of a slightly compressible water, of bulk modulus
$K_{w}$, then gives the Biot storage equation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\varepsilon}{K_{w}}\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}
  = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{c} + \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\frac{k}{\mu_{w}}\nabla p\Bigr),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{c} = \bigl(1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon\lambda\bigr)\,\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{s},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{q} = -\frac{k}{\mu_{w}}\nabla p .
  \label{eq:biot}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{c}$ is the volume of solid and water that the skeleton carries
through a unit area per unit time.  Where the skeleton compresses,
$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{c}<0$, the excess pressure rises; it then drives water out
through the pores and dissipates.  The permeability is the one that the flow
force~\eqref{eq:interaction} implies at low Reynolds number: equating the
Darcy pressure gradient $\mu_{w}\mathbf{q}/k$ with the force per unit volume
$n\,\mathbf{F}^{\,f}$ of the flow $\mathbf{q}/\varepsilon$ gives
\begin{equation}
  k = \frac{4}{3\cdot5.76}\;R_{32}^{2}\;\frac{\varepsilon^{4.7}}{1-\varepsilon},
  \label{eq:permeability}
\end{equation}
with $R_{32}$ the local Sauter mean radius, so that a fixed bed offers the same
resistance in the drained and in the coupled model.  For $\varepsilon=0.4$,
\eqref{eq:permeability} is about \num{1.3} times the Kozeny--Carman
permeability $d^{2}\varepsilon^{3}/(180(1-\varepsilon)^{2})$.  The excess pressure pushes on each particle in
proportion to the volume of mixture it represents,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\,p} = -\frac{V_{i}^{\,w}}{1-\varepsilon}\,\nabla p ,
  \label{eq:pressure-force}
\end{equation}
so that per unit volume the skeleton receives the whole gradient $-\nabla p$, as
in the mixture theory of saturated soils.  The flow force~\eqref{eq:interaction}
is then evaluated with the velocity of the water carried along by the skeleton,
$\mathbf{u}=\lambda\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{s}+U/\varepsilon\,\mathbf{e}_{y}$, so that it
acts only on particles that move relative to their neighbours.

\paragraph{Dense and dilute regions.}  A coarse-grid average cannot represent a
particle that moves alone through clear water: the grid would spread its
velocity over the whole cell and let the water follow it.  The share $\lambda$
of the pore water carried by the skeleton therefore depends on the solid
fraction $\phi=1-\varepsilon$: all of it in a dense packing ($\phi\ge0.5$), none of
it in a suspension ($\phi\le0.2$), and linearly in between.  A particle settling
alone in clear water is then dragged by still water exactly as in the drained
model, and the excess pressure there vanishes because the permeability of
nearly empty cells is very large.

\paragraph{Boundary conditions.}  The walls are impermeable.  Loaded platens are
permeable, like porous stones, so that the water can drain through them.  The
excess pressure vanishes above the free surface and next to open edges, where
the water can leave the domain.

\paragraph{Undrained response and scaling.}  Under a load applied faster than the
water can drain, the water carries the load: the excess pressure rises by the
fraction $B=1/(1+\varepsilon M/K_{w})$ of the stress increment, Skempton's
coefficient, with $M$ the constrained modulus of the skeleton.  Like the Young's
modulus of the particles, $K_{w}$ is scaled down (default
\qty{10}{\mega\pascal}) to keep the time step affordable
(Section~\ref{sec:water-numerics}); it only needs to stay large compared with $M$
for $B$ to remain close to one.  Grains of a few millimetres drain far faster
than a sand, because the permeability~\eqref{eq:permeability} grows with the
square of the grain size.  Since $k$ enters only as $k/\mu_{w}$, a viscosity $N$
times that of water makes the packing behave like a soil $N$ times less
permeable, for the flow force, the settling of particles and the pore pressure
alike.

\subsection{Assumptions and limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \emph{Darcy flow.}  The excess pressure drives a Darcy flow, which is
        valid at small pore Reynolds numbers; the inertial part of the drag
        enters only through the flow force~\eqref{eq:interaction}.
  \item \emph{Dense--dilute transition.}  The carried share $\lambda$ is a
        modelling assumption that joins the two limits in which the model is
        well founded, the porous medium and the isolated particle.
  \item \emph{Forces on the boundaries.}  The walls and platens are thin and
        surrounded by water on both sides when submerged, so the hydrostatic
        pressure on them cancels; they receive only the contact forces of the
        particles, i.e.\ the effective forces.
  \item \emph{Neglected interactions.}  The added mass, the history (Basset)
        force, lift and the torque of the water on a spinning particle are
        neglected, as are lubrication forces between approaching particles and
        capillary forces at the free surface.  Contacts keep their dry
        properties under water.
\end{itemize}

\section{Energy balance}
\label{sec:energy}

Although the code does not monitor it, the energy budget is a useful check.  The
stored energy of a contact is obtained by integrating the elastic parts of
\eqref{eq:hertz} and~\eqref{eq:Ft-trial},
\begin{equation}
  U_{n} = \int_{0}^{\delta_{n}} k_{n}s^{3/2}\,\mathrm{d}s
        = \frac{2}{5}k_{n}\delta_{n}^{5/2},
  \qquad
  U_{t} = \tfrac{1}{2}S_{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2},
  \label{eq:strain-energy}
\end{equation}
so the total mechanical energy of the system is
\begin{equation}
  E =
  \underbrace{\sum_{i}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}m_{i}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert^{2}
                          + \tfrac{1}{2}I_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}\Bigr)
              + \tfrac{1}{2}m_{p}v_{p}^{2}}_{\text{kinetic}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{i} m_{i}g\,y_{i}}_{\text{potential}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{\text{contacts}} \bigl(U_{n}+U_{t}\bigr)}_{\text{stored}} .
  \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Energy leaves the system through the two dashpots and through frictional
sliding,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}
  = \sum_{\text{contacts}}
    \Bigl(
      c_{n}v_{n}^{2}
      + c_{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert^{2}
      + \lVert\mathbf{F}_{t}\rVert\,
        \lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\text{slip}}\rVert
    \Bigr) \;\ge\; 0 ,
  \label{eq:dissipation}
\end{equation}
and enters through the work of the applied loads, $\dot W = \sum_{p} F(t)\,v_{p}$,
with $v_{p}$ positive into the domain.
The monotone dissipation~\eqref{eq:dissipation} is what allows the deposition
stage to converge to a static packing.

\section{Characteristic scales}
\label{sec:scales}

Three scales control the behaviour of the model and are used repeatedly in
Chapters~\ref{cha:numerical} and~\ref{cha:examples}.

\paragraph{Static overlap under a given load.}  Inverting~\eqref{eq:hertz},
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{n}
  = \left(\frac{3F_{n}}{4E^{*}\sqrt{R^{*}}}\right)^{2/3} .
  \label{eq:delta-static}
\end{equation}
The ratio $\delta_{n}/R$ is the key fidelity indicator of a soft-sphere
simulation and should remain of the order of one per cent.

\paragraph{Binary collision duration.}  For a Hertzian impact at relative
approach velocity $v_{0}$ the contact lasts~\cite{johnson1985}
\begin{equation}
  t_{c} \simeq 2.87
  \left(\frac{m^{*2}}{R^{*}\,{E^{*}}^{2}\,v_{0}}\right)^{1/5} .
  \label{eq:tc}
\end{equation}
The time step must resolve $t_{c}$ with several tens of increments; this is the
physical origin of the stability restriction discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:timestep}.

\paragraph{Free-fall and elastic wave scales.}  The time for a grain to fall its
own diameter, $\sqrt{4R/g}$, characterises the deposition stage, whereas the
shear-wave travel time across the smallest grain, $R_{\min}\sqrt{\rho/G}$,
characterises the stiffest response the mesh-free discretisation must follow.
The ratio of the two is the reason why DEM simulations of deposition are
expensive: the physics is slow, the stability limit is fast.

\section{Three-dimensional model}
\label{sec:3d}

The model of this chapter extends to three dimensions with the same contact law and the same boundary conditions.
The geometry decides which model runs: a polygon (\texttt{Geometry}) gives the planar model and a polyhedron (\texttt{Geometry3D}) the spatial one.
The $y$ axis stays vertical, so gravity, the water table and the deposition are unchanged, and $z$ is the depth.
A planar domain is therefore a slice $z=\text{const}$ of a spatial one, and \texttt{Geometry3D.extrude} turns a polygon into the prism it is a slice of.

\subsection{Degrees of freedom and equations of motion}

Particle $i$ has six degrees of freedom: the three coordinates of its centre, $\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})$, and three rotations, with the angular velocity vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}$.
The mass properties~\eqref{eq:massprops} are now those of the sphere itself.
Its moment of inertia is the same about every axis, so the gyroscopic term $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}\times I_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}$ vanishes and the Euler equation~\eqref{eq:euler} keeps its form, with vectors:
\begin{equation}
  I_{i}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}
    = \sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}} \mathbf{T}_{ij}
     + \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{i}} \mathbf{T}_{iw}.
  \label{eq:euler-3d}
\end{equation}
In the planar model every rotation is about $\mathbf{e}_{z}$, and the scalars $\omega_{i}$ and $T_{i}$ of Section~\ref{sec:eom} are the $z$ components of these vectors.

\subsection{Contact kinematics, force and torque}

The overlap~\eqref{eq:overlap} and the normal $\mathbf{n}$ of~\eqref{eq:frame} are unchanged, but the tangential quantities now lie anywhere in the contact plane normal to $\mathbf{n}$, not along a single tangent.
The material points of the two bodies at the contact, at $-R_{i}\mathbf{n}$ and $+R_{j}\mathbf{n}$ from their centres, slide over each other with the relative velocity
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{ij}
  = (\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{j})
    - \bigl(R_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}+R_{j}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\bigr)\times\mathbf{n},
  \label{eq:vrel-3d}
\end{equation}
which reduces to~\eqref{eq:vrel} for rotations about $\mathbf{e}_{z}$ and a normal in the $x$--$y$ plane, since then $\mathbf{e}_{z}\times\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{t}$.
The decomposition~\eqref{eq:vn-vt}, the tangential history~\eqref{eq:xi-project}--\eqref{eq:xi-accumulate} and the Coulomb return mapping~\eqref{eq:coulomb} apply unchanged to vectors of three components; the friction limit is a circular cone about $\mathbf{n}$.
The tangential force produces the torques
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{T}_{ij} = -R_{i}\,\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{F}_{t},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{T}_{ji} = -R_{j}\,\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{F}_{t},
  \label{eq:torque-3d}
\end{equation}
of which~\eqref{eq:Ti}--\eqref{eq:Tj} are the $z$ components.

\subsection{Rolling resistance}

In space the relative rotation $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}$ of two bodies in contact has two parts.
Its component along $\mathbf{n}$ twists the bodies about the normal, and its component in the contact plane rolls them over each other.
Only rolling is resisted, as in the model of Ai et al.~\cite{ai2011}; twisting is free.
The rolling spin and the rolling spring $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}$ are vectors in the contact plane,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}_{r}
  = (\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})
    - \bigl[(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{n}\bigr]\mathbf{n},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\theta}_{r} \;\leftarrow\;
  \boldsymbol{\theta}_{r} - (\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}\cdot\mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{n}
  + \boldsymbol{\omega}_{r}\,\Delta t ,
  \label{eq:theta-r-3d}
\end{equation}
where the stored spring is first projected onto the current contact plane, as the tangential spring is by~\eqref{eq:xi-project}.
The trial moment $\mathbf{M}_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}}=-k_{r}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}-c_{r}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{r}$ has the stiffness and the damping of~\eqref{eq:Mr-trial}, and the limit~\eqref{eq:rolling-limit} caps its magnitude:
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\mathbf{M}_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rVert > \mu_{r}R^{*}F_{n}:
  \qquad
  \mathbf{M}_{r} = \mu_{r}R^{*}F_{n}\,
    \frac{\mathbf{M}_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}}}{\lVert\mathbf{M}_{r}^{\mathrm{trial}}\rVert},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\theta}_{r} = -\frac{\mathbf{M}_{r}}{k_{r}} .
  \label{eq:rolling-limit-3d}
\end{equation}
Body $i$ receives $\mathbf{M}_{r}$ and body $j$ the opposite moment.
For a normal in the $x$--$y$ plane and rotations about $\mathbf{e}_{z}$ the projections change nothing, and the model reduces exactly to that of Section~\ref{sec:rolling}.

\subsection{Polyhedral domain}

The domain is a closed polyhedron whose faces are planar polygons, convex or not.
Each face is given by its vertices in order around it.
Every edge must be shared by exactly two faces that run along it in opposite directions, so that all faces are oriented alike.
Either orientation is accepted: the sign of the enclosed volume decides on which side of each face the inward normal $\mathbf{n}_{k}$ lies.
Each face carries one of the boundary conditions of Section~\ref{sec:domain}, and a load is applied on part of a side by splitting that side into coplanar faces, one of which is a loaded platen.

The contact point $\mathbf{c}$ of face $k$ is the point of the face closest to the particle centre.
It is the projection of the centre onto the plane of the face if that falls inside the polygon, and otherwise the closest point of its boundary, found with~\eqref{eq:closest-point} on each of its edges.
The overlap and the normal then follow from~\eqref{eq:edge-overlap}, so a particle beyond a face touches it at one of its edges or vertices.

\paragraph{Shared edges and vertices.}
The rule of Section~\ref{sec:walls} carries over to edges and vertices shared by any number of faces.
If the closest point of one solid face to a particle also lies on another solid face, the other face has a closest point at least as near, since that point is one of its own.
Either its closest point is nearer, and it alone represents the contact, or it is the same point, and of all the faces that report that point only the one with the lowest index keeps the contact.
A particle resting on the vertex where four floor faces meet therefore feels one contact, and so does a particle under the tip of a pyramidal body.
The same geometric test is applied to the edges of a polygon (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).

\paragraph{Loaded bodies.}
A loaded body is a set of faces that translates along $\mathbf{d}_{p}$.
By default $\mathbf{d}_{p}$ is the mean of the inward normals of the faces weighted by their areas, the counterpart of~\eqref{eq:platen-direction}.
To seat a body, its faces are swept along $\mathbf{d}_{p}$ until one of them touches a particle.
Seen from the body, the particle moves along $\mathbf{u}=-\mathbf{d}_{p}$ and first touches a face in its interior, at one of its edges or at one of its vertices.
The interior is touched when the distance of the centre from the plane of the face falls to the radius, as in~\eqref{eq:seat-line}, and the foot of the centre then lies inside the polygon.
A vertex is touched at the first root~\eqref{eq:seat-end}.
An edge from $\mathbf{a}$, with unit direction $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and length $L$, is touched when the centre reaches the cylinder of radius $R_{i}$ about it.
With $\mathbf{w}_{\perp}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{\perp}$ the components of $\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{a}$ and of $\mathbf{u}$ normal to the edge, that happens after the travel
\begin{equation}
  s_{\text{edge}}
  = \frac{-\mathbf{w}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{\perp}
          - \sqrt{(\mathbf{w}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{\perp})^{2}
                  - \lVert\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\rVert^{2}\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{w}_{\perp}\rVert^{2}-R_{i}^{2}\bigr)}}
         {\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\rVert^{2}},
  \label{eq:seat-edge}
\end{equation}
provided that the foot of the centre then lies on the edge, $0\le(\mathbf{w}+s_{\text{edge}}\mathbf{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}\le L$.
The seating travel is the smallest non-negative candidate over all faces of the body and all particles, as in two dimensions.

\subsection{Pore water}

The buoyancy~\eqref{eq:cap}, the flow force~\eqref{eq:interaction} and the coupled pore pressure~\eqref{eq:biot} are written for spheres in space and apply as they stand.
Only the porosity changes.
The volume porosity of a packing of spheres can be measured directly, so the conversion~\eqref{eq:porosity3d} from a planar layer of discs is not needed: $\varepsilon$ is averaged from the volumes of the spheres on a grid of cubic cells (Section~\ref{sec:3d-numerics}).

\subsection{Closed-form checks}

The checks of the planar model hold in space.
A sphere on a floor tilted about the oblique horizontal axis $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,0,1)$, with the same gradients as in Section~\ref{sec:rolling}, rolls straight down the slope, without slipping and without twisting.
It accelerates at \qtylist{3.0322;1.7688;0.5054}{\metre\per\second\squared} for $\mu_{r}=0$, $0.2$ and $0.4$, as~\eqref{eq:incline} requires, and stays put on the gentler gradient of $0.32$ with $\mu_{r}=0.4$.
A simulation whose particles move in a plane $z=\text{const}$ of a domain extruded from a polygon reproduces the planar simulation of that polygon to rounding error, rolling resistance and loaded bodies included.
