\chapter{Numerical solution}
\label{cha:numerical}

This chapter describes how the continuous model of Chapter~\ref{cha:formulation}
is turned into an algorithm: the data structures, the explicit time integrator
and its stability limit, the contact-detection strategy, the bookkeeping of the
tangential history, the deposition procedure that builds the initial packing,
and the resulting computational cost.

\section{Overview of the algorithm}
\label{sec:overview}

The simulation is advanced by a fixed-step explicit scheme.  One time step
consists of the operations listed below; the numbering is used as a reference in
the remainder of the chapter.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\textwidth}{\small
\textbf{Algorithm 1 -- one time step} (\texttt{DEMSimulation.step})
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em,nosep]
  \item \emph{First half kick} (uses the forces stored from the previous
        evaluation)\\
        $\mathbf{v}_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\mathbf{v}_{i}
          +\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{F}_{i}/m_{i}$,\quad
        $\omega_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\omega_{i}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}T_{i}/I_{i}$,\quad
        $v_{p}\!\leftarrow\!v_{p}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}a_{p}\bigl(t^{n}\bigr)$
  \item \emph{Drift}\\
        $\mathbf{x}_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\mathbf{x}_{i}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$,\quad
        $s_{p}\!\leftarrow\!s_{p}+\Delta t\,v_{p}$,\quad
        $t\!\leftarrow\!t+\Delta t$;\quad
        every 50 steps, remove the particles that left through an open edge
        (Section~\ref{sec:coupling})
  \item \emph{Force evaluation}
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,nosep,label=(\alph*)]
      \item reset $\mathbf{F}_{i}$, $T_{i}$; add gravity $-m_{i}g\,\mathbf{e}_{y}$
            and, with water, buoyancy and the pore pressure force; advance the
            pore pressure and evaluate the drag coefficients
            (Section~\ref{sec:water-numerics});
      \item rebuild the neighbour list if a particle has moved more than half
            the skin since the last build (Section~\ref{sec:verlet});
      \item for all candidate pairs at once: exact overlap
            test~\eqref{eq:overlap}, contact law of
            Section~\ref{sec:contact-summary}, assembly of
            $\pm\mathbf{F}_{ij}$ and $T_{ij},T_{ji}$;
      \item for all particles and all solid edges at once: closest
            point~\eqref{eq:closest-point}, removal of duplicate vertex
            contacts, contact law, and accumulation of the platen
            reactions~\eqref{eq:platen-reaction};
      \item store the updated tangential springs in place
            (Section~\ref{sec:history}).
    \end{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Second half kick} (uses the new forces and $F(t^{n+1})$)\\
        $\mathbf{v}_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\mathbf{v}_{i}
          +\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{F}_{i}/m_{i}$,\quad
        $\omega_{i}\!\leftarrow\!\omega_{i}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}T_{i}/I_{i}$,\quad
        $v_{p}\!\leftarrow\!v_{p}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}a_{p}\bigl(t^{n+1}\bigr)$
\end{enumerate}
}}
\end{center}

Three drivers chain these steps into the physical stages of the experiment:
\texttt{rain\_until\_full} (deposition, Section~\ref{sec:deposition}),
\texttt{rest} (relaxation) and \texttt{compress} (loading).  Every per-contact
operation of steps 3(c) and 3(d) is a NumPy array operation over all contacts
at once; no Python loop runs over particles or contacts.

\section{State representation}
\label{sec:state}

All particle data are held in contiguous \texttt{NumPy} arrays:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  Array & Shape & Content \\
  \midrule
  \texttt{pos}, \texttt{vel}, \texttt{force} & $(N,2)$ & $\mathbf{x}_{i}$, $\mathbf{v}_{i}$, $\mathbf{F}_{i}$ \\
  \texttt{omega}, \texttt{torque}            & $(N,)$  & $\omega_{i}$, $T_{i}$ \\
  \texttt{radius}, \texttt{mass}, \texttt{inertia} & $(N,)$ & $R_{i}$, $m_{i}$, $I_{i}$ \\
  \texttt{inv\_mass}, \texttt{inv\_inertia}  & $(N,)$  & $1/m_{i}$, $1/I_{i}$ \\
  neighbour list \texttt{first}, \texttt{second} & $(P,)$ & candidate pairs $(i,j)$, $i<j$ \\
  neighbour list \texttt{spring}             & $(P,2)$ & $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ of each candidate pair \\
  wall springs                               & $(N,S,2)$ & $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ of each particle--edge pair \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $P$ is the length of the neighbour list and $S$ the number of solid edges.
In three dimensions the vectors have three components and the angular quantities become vectors (Section~\ref{sec:3d-numerics}).

Two implementation details matter.  First, the reciprocals $1/m_{i}$ and
$1/I_{i}$ are cached because they are used twice per step, in the two half
kicks.  Second, new particles are always \emph{appended} at the end of the
arrays, so existing indices, and with them the pair keys of
Section~\ref{sec:history}, stay valid across insertions.  Particles that leave
through an open edge are removed and the others renumbered; the renumbering
preserves their order, so the tangential history is carried over.  Whenever the
radius array changes, $m_{i}$, $I_{i}$ and their reciprocals are recomputed
from~\eqref{eq:massprops}.

\section{Time integration}
\label{sec:integration}

\subsection{Velocity-Verlet scheme}

The equations of motion~\eqref{eq:newton}--\eqref{eq:euler} are integrated with
the velocity-Verlet algorithm~\cite{swope1982,allen2017} in its kick--drift--kick
form:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2} &= \mathbf{v}^{\,n}
    + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,\frac{\mathbf{F}^{\,n}}{m},
    \label{eq:vv1}\\
  \mathbf{x}^{\,n+1} &= \mathbf{x}^{\,n} + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2},
    \label{eq:vv2}\\
  \mathbf{F}^{\,n+1} &= \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\,n+1},
                        \mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2},
                        \boldsymbol{\xi}^{\,n+1}\bigr),
    \label{eq:vv3}\\
  \mathbf{v}^{\,n+1} &= \mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2}
    + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,\frac{\mathbf{F}^{\,n+1}}{m}.
    \label{eq:vv4}
\end{align}
The rotational degrees of freedom and the platen degree of freedom are advanced
by the same three-stage pattern, with $T_{i}/I_{i}$ and
$a_{p}=\bigl(F(t)-F_{p}+\chi\,m_{p}\,\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{p}\bigr)/m_{p}$ in place of $\mathbf{F}/m$.

The scheme is explicit, requires a single force evaluation per step, stores no
history beyond the current state, is time reversible, and is second-order
accurate: the local truncation error is $O(\Delta t^{3})$ in position and the
global error is $O(\Delta t^{2})$.  For conservative forces it is symplectic,
so the energy error remains bounded rather than drifting -- an essential
property for the long deposition runs.

\subsection{Velocity-dependent forces}

The contact force is not conservative: it depends on $\mathbf{v}$ through the
dashpots~\eqref{eq:cn} and~\eqref{eq:Ft-trial}.  In~\eqref{eq:vv3} it is
evaluated at the half-step velocity $\mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2}$, which is the only
velocity available at that point without an implicit solve or an extra
iteration.  Since
$\mathbf{v}^{\,n+1/2}=\mathbf{v}^{\,n+1}-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{a}^{\,n+1}
 +O(\Delta t^{2})$, the dissipative part of the force carries an $O(\Delta t)$
error, so the scheme is formally first order in the damping contribution and
strictly symplectic only in the limit $\beta\to0$.  The resulting bias is
proportional to $\beta$ and to $\Delta t/t_{c}$; with the values used here
($\beta=0.28$, $\Delta t/t_{c}\approx0.03$) it is well below the other
modelling errors.  The same approximation is made by most production DEM
codes~\cite{silbert2001,kloss2012}.

\subsection{Reference integrator for verification}

The class \texttt{VelocityVerlet} implements~\eqref{eq:vv1}--\eqref{eq:vv4} for
a generic acceleration callback $\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})$.  It is not
used by the production loop -- which fuses the kicks with the force evaluation
to avoid recomputing $\mathbf{F}^{\,n}$ -- but it exposes the integrator to unit
testing against analytical solutions (Section~\ref{sec:unit-tests}).

\section{Stable time step}
\label{sec:timestep}

Being explicit, the integrator is only conditionally stable.  The binding
restriction is the fastest elastic response of the assembly, which is the
propagation of a surface (Rayleigh) wave across the \emph{smallest} particle.
The Rayleigh critical time step is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  T_{R} = \frac{\pi R_{\min}}{0.1631\,\nu + 0.8766}
          \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{G}} \;}
  \label{eq:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is the standard rational approximation of the ratio
between the Rayleigh and the shear wave speeds~\cite{osullivan2004}.  The code
uses a safety fraction
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t = \alpha\,T_{R},
  \qquad \alpha = 0.2 ,
  \label{eq:dt}
\end{equation}
which lies in the middle of the range $0.1$--$0.3$ recommended in the
literature for dense assemblies with coordination numbers of four to six.  A
user-supplied $\Delta t$ overrides~\eqref{eq:dt}.

Three remarks on~\eqref{eq:rayleigh}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item It scales with $R_{\min}$, not with the mean radius.  A single very
        small grain therefore penalises the whole simulation, which is why the
        size ratio $R_{\max}/R_{\min}$ should be kept moderate.
  \item It scales with $\sqrt{\rho/G}$, i.e.\ with the inverse shear-wave
        speed.  Reducing $E$ (and hence $G$) by a factor of $100$ increases the
        admissible time step by a factor of $10$; this "artificial softening" is
        the standard device that makes DEM of stiff materials affordable, at
        the cost of larger overlaps (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
  \item It is independent of the load.  A complementary check is that
        $\Delta t$ must resolve the collision time~\eqref{eq:tc}; both criteria
        are evaluated in Table~\ref{tab:dt} and the Rayleigh one is the more
        restrictive.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contact detection}
\label{sec:broadphase}

\subsection{Uniform grid (cell list)}

A naive search over all $\tfrac{1}{2}N(N-1)$ pairs costs $O(N^{2})$ per step and
is unaffordable.  Instead the domain is covered by a uniform grid of square
cells of side
\begin{equation}
  h = 2R_{\max} + s,
  \label{eq:cellsize}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the skin distance of the neighbour list
(Section~\ref{sec:verlet}), and every particle is binned into the cell
\begin{equation}
  c(\mathbf{x}_{i}) = \bigl(\lfloor (x_{i}-x_{\min})/h\rfloor,\;
                            \lfloor (y_{i}-y_{\min})/h\rfloor\bigr)
                    \in \mathbb{Z}^{2},
  \label{eq:cellindex}
\end{equation}
counted from the lower left corner of the particle cloud.  The cells are
numbered linearly and the particles sorted by cell number, so the members of any
cell are found by a binary search in the sorted array.  Only occupied cells take
memory, so the cost is $O(N)$ regardless of how empty the domain is -- an
important property during deposition, when the bed occupies a small and growing
fraction of the domain.

\paragraph{Correctness.}  The choice~\eqref{eq:cellsize} guarantees that no
candidate pair is missed.  If the surfaces of $i$ and $j$ are less than $s$
apart then $d_{ij}<R_{i}+R_{j}+s\le h$, hence $\lvert x_{i}-x_{j}\rvert<h$ and
$\lvert y_{i}-y_{j}\rvert<h$, so their cell indices differ by at most one in
each direction.  Searching a particle's own cell and its eight neighbours is
therefore exhaustive.

\paragraph{Half stencil.}  To visit every unordered pair exactly once, the
search combines the pairs inside each cell with the pairs between the cell and
only \emph{half} of its neighbours,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{O} = \bigl\{(1,0),\ (0,1),\ (1,1),\ (-1,1)\bigr\},
  \label{eq:stencil}
\end{equation}
the remaining four offsets being the negatives of these and therefore already
covered when the neighbouring cell is processed.  This halves the number of
candidate pairs and, more importantly, removes the risk of applying a contact
force twice.  For each offset the candidates of all particles are generated in
one vectorised operation, by repeating every particle index as many times as the
target cell has members.

\paragraph{Distance filter.}  The candidates are ordered as $(i,j)$ with $i<j$,
only those with $d_{ij}<R_{i}+R_{j}+s$ are kept, and the result is sorted by $i$
and then by $j$.

\subsection{Verlet neighbour list}
\label{sec:verlet}

The candidate pairs are kept as a \emph{neighbour list} and reused over many
steps.  If no particle has moved by more than $s/2$ since the list was built, no
two particles can have approached each other by more than $s$, so every pair now
in contact was within the skin at build time and is in the list.  The list is
therefore rebuilt only when
\begin{equation}
  \max_{i}\,\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{build}}\rVert
  > \tfrac{1}{2}s ,
  \label{eq:rebuild}
\end{equation}
or when particles have been added or removed.  The criterion is exact, so the
list never loses a contact.  At every step only the narrow phase, the exact
test~\eqref{eq:overlap}, runs over the list, and non-overlapping candidates are
discarded before any constitutive evaluation.  The skin is set as a fraction of
$R_{\min}$ (\texttt{GlobalSettings.neighbour\_skin}, default $0.5$): a larger
skin rebuilds less often but tests more pairs per step.  During quasi-static
loading the particles barely move and the list is hardly ever rebuilt.

\subsection{Cost and the effect of polydispersity}

With a cell size fixed by the largest particle, the expected number of particles
per cell is
\begin{equation}
  n_{\text{cell}} \sim \phi \left(\frac{h}{2\bar{R}}\right)^{2}
  \quad\text{and, in the worst case,}\quad
  n_{\text{cell}}^{\max} \sim
  \left(\frac{R_{\max}}{R_{\min}}\right)^{2},
\end{equation}
so the number of pairs examined while building the list grows quadratically
with the size ratio; the distance filter then reduces them to the pairs within
the skin.  The cost of one step is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{O}\bigl(N\log N\bigr)\ \text{(sorting, at rebuilds only)}
  + \mathcal{O}\bigl(P\bigr)\ \text{(narrow phase)}
  + \mathcal{O}\bigl(N S\bigr)\ \text{(solid edges)},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ essentially linear in $N$.  Compared with a loop over the pairs in Python,
the vectorised evaluation is about an order of magnitude faster for a few
hundred particles, and the gain grows with $N$.

\section{Tangential-history bookkeeping}
\label{sec:history}

The tangential spring $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ and the rolling spring $\theta_{r}$
must persist as long as the contact exists and must disappear with it.  They
are stored next to the pair they belong to:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Storage & Contact \\
  \midrule
  \texttt{NeighbourList.spring[k]} & particle pair $k$ of the neighbour list, $(i,j)$ with $i<j$ \\
  \texttt{NeighbourList.rolling[k]} & the same pair, rolling spring \\
  wall springs \texttt{[i, s]} & particle $i$ -- solid edge $s$ (wall or platen), both springs \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The rolling springs follow every rule below exactly like the tangential ones.

This storage provides the three required behaviours:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \textbf{Creation.}  A pair enters the neighbour list with
        $\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{0}$, so a contact that closes for the first
        time starts from zero, as required.
  \item \textbf{Persistence.}  A contact that survives keeps its spring, which
        is projected and incremented
        by~\eqref{eq:xi-project}--\eqref{eq:xi-accumulate}.  When the neighbour
        list is rebuilt, each pair is identified by the integer key
        $i\cdot 2^{32}+j$; because both the old and the new list are sorted by
        key, the springs of the surviving pairs are carried over by a single
        binary search.
  \item \textbf{Deletion.}  The spring of a pair that is not in contact is reset
        to zero at every step, so a later re-closure starts afresh.
\end{enumerate}
The keys are index based, which is safe because indices are only appended or
renumbered in order (Section~\ref{sec:state}).

Two consistency requirements follow from~\eqref{eq:xi-accumulate}: the sliding
of a time step must be accumulated exactly once, and over the integration step
$\Delta t$.  Only the force evaluation that follows a drift accumulates.  When
the forces of an unchanged state are evaluated again -- before the first step,
after particles were inserted, or when the platens are released -- the
evaluation uses a zero increment, so the springs are only projected onto the
current tangent plane.  (The first version of the code accumulated in these
re-evaluations too, which added a spurious increment
$\mathbf{v}_{t}\Delta t$ to every closed contact at every wave of the
deposition.)

\section{Boundary and platen coupling}
\label{sec:coupling}

Edge contacts are evaluated for all particles and all solid edges at once,
using~\eqref{eq:closest-point}--\eqref{eq:edge-overlap} and the degenerate
effective quantities~\eqref{eq:wall-effective}.  The cost is $O(NS)$ with a very
small constant; the overlap test rejects the interior particles immediately.
Duplicate vertex contacts are removed with the rule of Section~\ref{sec:walls}, tested geometrically.
For a particle that touches more than one solid edge, a contact whose closest point also lies on another of those edges is dropped when that edge has a closer point of its own; when both report the same point, only the edge with the lower index keeps it.
The test needs no knowledge of which edges are neighbours, so it holds as well for the edges of a loaded body that has moved away from its neighbours, and for the faces of a polyhedron (Section~\ref{sec:3d}).

Particles that leave the domain through an open edge are removed once their
centre lies outside the polygon at a distance larger than an escape margin
(\texttt{GlobalSettings.escape\_margin}, default four largest radii).  The
polygon test, an even--odd crossing count, is run every 50 steps; a particle
that has left interacts with nothing, so removing it slightly late is harmless.

The platens are integrated with the same velocity-Verlet pattern as the
particles, in a \emph{staggered} (explicit, one-way at each half step) fashion:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item the first half kick uses the reaction $F_{p}$ computed at the end of the
        previous step and the load $F(t^{n})$;
  \item the drift moves the platen with $v_{p}^{\,n+1/2}$, so the particles see
        the new platen position and the half-step platen velocity
        in~\eqref{eq:vrel-platen};
  \item the force evaluation accumulates the new reaction $F_{p}^{\,n+1}$
        of~\eqref{eq:platen-reaction};
  \item the second half kick uses $F_{p}^{\,n+1}$ and $F(t^{n+1})$.
\end{itemize}
The coupling is therefore fully explicit and adds no stability restriction of
its own provided that the platen is not much lighter than the grains it
touches; a very light platen would behave like an extra particle with a very
small mass and would require a smaller $\Delta t$ through the $m^{*}$
in~\eqref{eq:cn}.  A practical rule is $m_{p}\gtrsim 10\,m_{\max}$; the
reference case uses $m_{p}=\qty{0.5}{\kilogram}$ against
$m_{\max}=\qty{0.084}{\kilogram}$.

The edges of a loaded body are stored with the walls as solid edges; at every
evaluation they are shifted by $s_{p}\mathbf{d}_{p}$ and given the velocity
$v_{p}\mathbf{d}_{p}$, and the reactions of all their contacts are summed into
the single $F_{p}$ of their body.  Until the load is applied a body acts as a
fixed wall.  The driver
\texttt{compress} seats every platen
with~\eqref{eq:seat-line}--\eqref{eq:seat-end}, releases it
and starts the load clock \texttt{load\_time}, which is advanced only while the
platens are active, so $F(t)$ starts from zero when loading begins,
independently of how long the deposition took.

\section{Initial configuration and deposition}
\label{sec:deposition}

A DEM result is only as good as its initial packing.  Two generators are
provided.

\subsection{Loose lattice generator}

\texttt{generate\_lattice\_particles} places $N$ particles on a jittered square
lattice inside the domain.  The lattice, of pitch
\begin{equation}
  s_{\ell} = 2 R_{\max}\,\sigma, \qquad \sigma \ge 1
  \quad(\text{default } \sigma = 1.15),
\end{equation}
covers the bounding box of the polygon, and only the sites that lie inside the
polygon with a clearance of at least $R_{\max}+\sqrt{2}\,\Delta_{\text{jitter}}$
from every edge are used, lowest rows first.  A uniform random offset of
amplitude
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\text{jitter}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,j\,\bigl(s_{\ell} - 2R_{\max}\bigr),
  \qquad 0 \le j \le 1 \quad (\text{default } j = 0.25),
\end{equation}
is added to both coordinates.  The bounds are chosen so that \emph{no} initial
overlap can be created, between particles or with an edge, whatever the random
draw: the residual clearance $s_{\ell}-2R_{\max}$ between sites is never fully
consumed, and the edge clearance allows for a diagonal displacement of
$\sqrt{2}\,\Delta_{\text{jitter}}$.  Breaking the lattice symmetry matters,
because a perfect lattice collapses into a crystalline packing with spurious
anisotropy.  Radii are drawn from~\eqref{eq:radii} with an explicit seed, so
packings are reproducible.  If fewer sites fit than particles are requested, the
generator raises an error instead of placing particles outside the domain.

\subsection{Wave-by-wave raining}

The driver used for the reference simulation, \texttt{rain\_until\_full},
starts from an \emph{empty} domain and fills it adaptively.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\textwidth}{\small
\textbf{Algorithm 2 -- raining until full} (\texttt{rain\_until\_full})
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em,nosep]
  \item $w \leftarrow 0$ (counter of consecutive empty waves),
        $\mathcal{Q}\leftarrow\emptyset$ (queue of radii still to be placed)
  \item \textbf{while} $w < w_{\max}$ \textbf{and} $N < N_{\max}$
        \textbf{and} steps $<$ cap:
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,nosep,label=(\alph*)]
      \item draw radii from~\eqref{eq:radii} until $\mathcal{Q}$ holds $n_{b}$
            of them;
      \item for each radius $R$ in $\mathcal{Q}$, largest first, try $n_{a}$
            random centres in the insertion band
            $y\in[y_{\text{lo}},y_{\text{hi}}]$; a candidate is accepted if it
            lies inside the polygon, at least $R$ from every edge and platen,
            and overlaps neither an existing particle near the band nor a
            particle already accepted in this wave;
      \item append the accepted particles with velocity
            $(0,-v_{\text{rain}})$; the radii that could not be placed stay in
            $\mathcal{Q}$;
      \item $w \leftarrow 0$ if at least one particle was inserted, else
            $w \leftarrow w+1$;
      \item advance $n_{s}$ time steps (Algorithm 1).
    \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{final settling}: advance up to $n_{\text{settle}}$ steps,
        stopping as soon as the quiescence criterion~\eqref{eq:quiet} is met.
\end{enumerate}
}}
\end{center}

By default the insertion band lies one largest radius below the top of the
polygon, $[y_{\max}-2R_{\max},\,y_{\max}-R_{\max}]$; it can be set explicitly,
for instance to leave freeboard below an open top, or when the top of the
polygon is a narrow apex.  The rejection test is restricted to the particles
whose centres lie above $y_{\text{lo}}-2R_{\max}$, which keeps the insertion
cost proportional to the number of grains near the surface rather than to $N$.
The small downward seed velocity $v_{\text{rain}}$ removes the initial free-fall
transient and makes the arrival of successive waves more regular.  The
termination logic is purely geometric: when the growing bed reaches the
insertion band, rejection sampling can no longer place anything, $w$ reaches
$w_{\max}$ and the loop exits.  The number of particles is therefore an
\emph{output} of the procedure, determined by the geometry and the size
distribution, not an input.

\paragraph{Honouring the size distribution.}  A radius is drawn once and kept in
the queue until it is placed.  The first version of the code drew a fresh radius
at every attempt; because a large candidate is far more likely to overlap an
existing grain, that biased the packing towards small particles.  Retrying the
same radius, largest first, removes the bias; only the at most $n_{b}$ radii
still queued when the domain is full are never placed.  For the reference
set-up the deposited radii now have a mean of \qty{12.25}{\milli\metre} and a
standard deviation of \qty{4.67}{\milli\metre}, against
\qty{12.0}{\milli\metre} and \qty{4.62}{\milli\metre} for the target
distribution $\mathcal{U}(\qty{4}{\milli\metre},\qty{20}{\milli\metre})$, and
the box holds \num{179} particles instead of \num{460}.

\subsection{Quiescence criterion}

Deposition and settling terminate on the mean translational kinetic energy per
particle,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \bar{E}_{k}
  = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac{1}{2}m_{i}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert^{2}
  \;<\; \epsilon \;}
  \label{eq:quiet}
\end{equation}
combined with a minimum number of steps, so that the criterion cannot be
satisfied trivially at $t=0$ when every particle is still at rest.  The
normalisation by $N$ makes the threshold independent of how many particles have
been deposited, which is essential here because $N$ grows during the run.  A
useful way to choose $\epsilon$ is to compare it with the potential energy of a
grain displaced by a small fraction of its own radius: with
$\epsilon=\qty{1e-6}{\joule}$ and a mean grain mass of about
\qty{2e-2}{\kilogram}, the corresponding root-mean-square velocity is
$\sqrt{2\epsilon/\bar{m}}\approx\qty{1e-2}{\metre\per\second}$, i.e.\ two orders
of magnitude below the raining velocity.

\section{Pore water}
\label{sec:water-numerics}

\paragraph{Porosity grid.}  The local porosity is averaged on a uniform grid of
square cells of side $h_{\varepsilon}$, by default two largest particle
diameters ($4R_{\max}$), so that each cell holds several particles.  With the
cloud-in-cell weights $w_{ik}$ -- the bilinear weights of the four grid nodes $k$
around the centre of particle $i$ -- every particle spreads its disc area over
the nodes, and the area of the domain around each node, $A^{d}_{k}$, is spread
in the same way from a fine sampling of the domain ($8\times8$ points per cell)
computed once.  The disc-layer porosity at particle $i$ is then
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_{2D,i} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{k} w_{ik}\,A^{s}_{k}}{\sum_{k} w_{ik}\,A^{d}_{k}},
  \qquad A^{s}_{k} = \sum_{j} w_{jk}\,\pi R_{j}^{2}.
  \label{eq:cic}
\end{equation}
Interpolating the solid and the domain areas separately keeps nodes that lie
outside the domain, or are cut by a wall, from biasing the result: a regular
lattice gives its exact porosity also next to the walls.  The value is converted
with~\eqref{eq:porosity3d} and bounded below by $\varepsilon_{\min}=0.2$, because
overlapping particles can make a dense packing look denser than any real one.

\paragraph{Implicit drag.}  The flow force~\eqref{eq:interaction} is written as
$\beta_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{i})$, with a coefficient $\beta_{i}$ that the
correlation~\eqref{eq:drag-cd} keeps finite as the relative speed vanishes.  The
coefficient and the water velocity are evaluated with the other forces, and the
two half kicks of Algorithm~1 take the velocity at their end,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_{i} \leftarrow
  \frac{\mathbf{v}_{i} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2m_{i}}\bigl(\mathbf{F}_{i}+\beta_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\bigr)}
       {1+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2m_{i}}\beta_{i}},
  \label{eq:implicit-drag}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_{i}$ holds all the other forces.  The update is stable however
strong the drag, which matters for fine grains and for viscous water: in a dense
packing at vanishing Reynolds number the drag would otherwise limit the time
step to about $m_{i}\varepsilon^{3.7}/(5.76\pi\mu_{w}R_{i})$.  At steady state it
reproduces the force balance exactly, which is why the settling velocities of
Section~\ref{sec:water-examples} agree with the drag balance to a few parts in
$10^{4}$.

\paragraph{Pore pressure.}  For coupled water every grid node with a domain area
is a control volume for~\eqref{eq:biot}.  Two neighbouring nodes exchange water
through a face of length $h_{\varepsilon}$ times the fraction of the face that
lies in the domain, with the harmonic mean of their mobilities $k/\mu_{w}$, as
for two resistances in series; faces to nodes outside the domain are closed,
which makes the walls impermeable.  The carried flux $\mathbf{c}$ at a node is
built from the solid-volume-weighted mean velocity of the particles around it,
and its net outflow from each control volume is the source of~\eqref{eq:biot}.
The diffusion is integrated with backward Euler,
\begin{equation}
  \Bigl(\frac{S}{\Delta t}+L\Bigr)\,\mathbf{p}^{\,n+1}
  = \frac{S}{\Delta t}\,\mathbf{p}^{\,n} - \mathbf{D},
  \label{eq:pressure-update}
\end{equation}
with the storage $S=\mathrm{diag}(A^{d}_{k}\varepsilon_{k}/K_{w})$, the
conductance matrix $L$ and the outflow $\mathbf{D}$; nodes above the free surface
or next to an open edge are held at $p=0$.  The update is unconditionally stable,
whatever the permeability.  The system is symmetric and positive definite and is
solved at every step by conjugate gradients with a diagonal preconditioner,
started from the previous pressure, to a relative residual of $10^{-10}$.  The
operator is applied face by face, so the solver needs no matrix, its cost grows
only linearly with the number of nodes, and it avoids the dense linear algebra
library, whose multithreaded routines can be slower than the whole time step on
systems of a few hundred unknowns.  The porosity and the permeability are
therefore always current.  The pressure gradient at each node
is the mean of the gradients across its open faces, and is interpolated to the
particles with the cloud-in-cell weights.  Like the tangential springs, the
pressure advances only in the force evaluation that follows a drift.  On a
column drained at the top, the solver reproduces Terzaghi's solution for the
dissipation of a uniform excess pressure to within \num{1}\% of the initial
value with ten cells over the column (Section~\ref{sec:water-examples}).

\paragraph{Time step of the coupled model.}  The pressure force is explicit, so
two time scales of the coupling must be resolved.  An undrained pressure wave
travels at $c=\sqrt{K_{w}/(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{s})}$, which the step must
follow across a cell; and in viscous water the drained pressure stops a
collective motion of the skeleton within the relaxation time
$\tau_{p}=(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{s}k/\mu_{w}$.  The time step is therefore limited to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t \le \min\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,h_{\varepsilon}\big/c(\varepsilon_{\min}),\;
                        \tau_{p}(R_{\min},\,\varepsilon=0.35)\Bigr),
  \label{eq:coupled-dt}
\end{equation}
the second term for the smallest particles in a dense packing.  For the
consolidation example of Section~\ref{sec:water-examples}
($K_{w}=\qty{10}{\mega\pascal}$, $\mu_{w}=\qty{1}{\pascal\second}$, $R_{\min}=\qty{4}{\milli\metre}$) the two limits are
\qty{7.6e-5}{\second} and \qty{6.7e-5}{\second}, against a Rayleigh step of
\qty{9.7e-5}{\second}.  A user-supplied $\Delta t$ that violates them is
rejected; the default is reduced instead.  On a dense packing of \num{500}
particles, drained water adds about a third to the cost of a step.  A coupled
step costs about two and a half times a drained one, mostly for the pressure
solution, which takes some \num{25} conjugate-gradient iterations in viscous
water and some \num{55} in water of ordinary viscosity, where the excess pressure
diffuses fastest.

\paragraph{Stages.}  The water can be added, changed or removed between stages
with \texttt{set\_water}, e.g.\ to deposit a packing in drained water and then
load it with coupled water, or to start a seepage flow after deposition.  The
level and the superficial velocity are functions of the simulation time, so a
ramp that should start now is written relative to the current time.  The force
that the particles exert on every edge of the domain, \texttt{edge\_forces}, is
the effective force on that wall, and \texttt{excess\_pore\_pressure} samples the
excess pore pressure at any point.

\section{Recording and post-processing}
\label{sec:recording}

Two output streams are collected at user-defined intervals.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[Frames] \texttt{Frame} records
        $\bigl(t,\ \mathbf{x}_{i},\ R_{i},\ \lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert,\
        \text{edges of the loaded bodies},\ \text{phase},\ y_{w}\bigr)$ every
        \texttt{record\_every} steps.  Positions and radii are \emph{copied},
        not referenced, because the underlying arrays are overwritten in place
        and reallocated when particles are added or removed.  The phase label
        (\texttt{deposition}, \texttt{settling}, \texttt{loading}) is carried
        along so that the animation can be annotated.
  \item[Edge forces] \texttt{edge\_forces}, the force the particles exert on every
        edge of the domain at the last evaluation, available at any time; with
        \texttt{fluid\_force} and \texttt{porosity}, the force of the water on
        every particle and its local porosity.
  \item[Load curves] one \texttt{LoadCurve} per platen, with
        $\bigl(t,\ F(t),\ u_{p},\ F_{p}\bigr)$ recorded during the loading stage
        only, starting with the seated state at $t=0$.  Comparing $F(t)$ with
        $F_{p}$ is the simplest available check of quasi-staticity: the two
        curves coincide when the platen inertia is negligible, and separate when
        the loading is too fast.
\end{description}

The number of stored frames, not the number of time steps, dominates the memory
footprint of a long run: each frame holds $O(N)$ floating-point numbers.  The
module \texttt{plotting} turns the frames into an animation and the load curves
into load--time and load--displacement plots.

\section{Verification strategy}
\label{sec:verification}

The implementation is verified at three levels, in increasing order of
integration:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{Unit level}: each closed-form expression
        (\eqref{eq:moduli}, \eqref{eq:hertz}, \eqref{eq:coulomb}) and the
        integrator are compared with analytical results
        (Section~\ref{sec:unit-tests});
  \item \emph{Component level}: single-contact experiments -- a bouncing grain,
        a sliding grain -- reproduce the prescribed restitution and friction
        coefficients (Section~\ref{sec:component-tests});
  \item \emph{System level}: the full deposition-and-compression experiment is
        checked for global consistency, in particular the equilibrium condition
        $F_{p}\to F(t)$ (Section~\ref{sec:reference-run}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Validity, accuracy and practical guidance}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{The overlap criterion}

The Hertz--Mindlin law is a linear-elastic, half-space solution.  Its derivation
assumes that the contact radius is small compared with the sphere radius,
$a\ll R$, which through~\eqref{eq:contact-radius} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \frac{\delta_{n}}{R} \ll 1,
  \qquad\text{in practice}\qquad
  \frac{\delta_{n}}{R}\lesssim 1\% \;}
  \label{eq:overlap-criterion}
\end{equation}
Combining~\eqref{eq:delta-static} with the fact that a load $F$ applied to a
platen of width $B$ is carried by roughly $n_{c}\approx B/(2\bar{R})$ contacts
gives an \emph{a priori} estimate of the overlap in the top layer,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta_{n}}{\bar{R}}
  \approx \frac{1}{\bar{R}}
  \left(\frac{3F}{4 n_{c} E^{*}\sqrt{\bar{R}/2}}\right)^{2/3}.
  \label{eq:overlap-estimate}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:overlap} evaluates~\eqref{eq:overlap-estimate} for the reference
material.  It shows that with $E=\qty{5}{\mega\pascal}$ the model is
quantitatively trustworthy only up to a few tens of newtons; beyond that the
grains interpenetrate visibly and the computed stiffness of the packing is no
longer that of a Hertzian assembly.  This is a limitation of the chosen
\emph{parameters}, not of the implementation, and it is quantified further in
Section~\ref{sec:load-level}: in the reference run the measured mean overlap of
the particles touching the platen is \num{8.7}\% of their radius at
\qty{100}{\newton} and \num{42.5}\% at \qty{1000}{\newton}, close to the
estimates of Table~\ref{tab:overlap}.

\subsection{Artificial softening}

Real sand or glass has $E\sim\qty{50}{\giga\pascal}$; the reference material
uses $\qty{5}{\mega\pascal}$, four orders of magnitude softer.  This is the usual
compromise: from~\eqref{eq:rayleigh}, $\Delta t \propto E^{-1/2}$, so restoring a
realistic stiffness would divide the time step by about $100$ and multiply the
run time accordingly.  From~\eqref{eq:delta-static},
$\delta_{n}\propto E^{-2/3}$, so the softening inflates the overlaps by a factor
of about $460$ at a given load.  When a quantitative stress--strain response is
required, the modulus must be raised until the results become insensitive to it,
which is the softening-convergence study that any serious application should
include.

\subsection{Other sources of error}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \emph{Time-step error}: halving $\Delta t$ should change the packing
        statistics by less than the run-to-run scatter due to the random seed.
        Because a granular assembly is chaotic, individual trajectories will
        never converge; only ensemble quantities (porosity, coordination number,
        load--displacement curve) do.
  \item \emph{Tangential history rotation}: the projection
        \eqref{eq:xi-project} does not conserve the magnitude of
        $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ when the contact plane rotates quickly; the error is
        $O(\Delta t^{2})$ per step but accumulates in long-lived rolling
        contacts.
  \item \emph{Particle shape}: a two-dimensional assembly of round particles
        is unrealistically free to roll, which under-predicts the shear
        strength and the angle of repose.  The rolling resistance of
        Section~\ref{sec:rolling} restores much of that strength, but
        $\mu_{r}$ is a calibration parameter, not a measured property: it
        should be fitted to the angle of repose or to a shear test of the
        real material.  The ballast study of
        Section~\ref{sec:ballast} shows how strongly the response depends on
        it.
  \item \emph{Boundary effects}: with $B/\bar{d}\approx 8$ grain diameters
        across, wall friction carries a large share of the load (Janssen
        effect).  In the reference run the side walls carry \num{22}\% of the
        self-weight and \qtyrange{56}{62}{\percent} of the load plus weight
        under the platen (Section~\ref{sec:reference-run}), so the measured
        platen reaction is not a pure material property.  A wider box or periodic lateral boundaries are
        required for constitutive characterisation.
  \item \emph{Two dimensions}: force chains in 2D are more percolating and the
        packing fraction is systematically higher than in 3D; results transfer
        qualitatively, not quantitatively.
        The three-dimensional model of Section~\ref{sec:3d} removes this limitation, at the cost of many more particles for a domain of the same size.
\end{itemize}

\section{Three-dimensional implementation}
\label{sec:3d-numerics}

The algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:overview} is the same in three dimensions; only the shapes of the arrays and the geometric queries change.

\paragraph{State.}
The arrays of Section~\ref{sec:state} gain a third column: \texttt{pos}, \texttt{vel} and \texttt{force} have the shape $(N,3)$, and \texttt{omega} and \texttt{torque} hold vectors, also of shape $(N,3)$.
The rolling springs of the neighbour list and of the walls become vectors as well.
A few helpers evaluate the cross products of~\eqref{eq:vrel-3d} and~\eqref{eq:torque-3d} as vectors in 3D and as their scalar $z$ components in 2D, with exactly the arithmetic of the planar model, whose results therefore do not change by a single bit.

\paragraph{Contact detection.}
The cell grid of Section~\ref{sec:broadphase} has cubic cells of side~\eqref{eq:cellsize}.
Each cell is compared with itself and with 13 of its 26 neighbours: the 9 cells of the layer above, the 3 cells of the row above in its own layer and the next cell of its own row.
This visits every unordered pair of cells once.
The neighbour list and its rebuild criterion are unchanged.

\paragraph{Face contacts.}
Only particles closer to the plane of a face than their radius can touch it.
The signed heights of all particles above all face planes are computed first, at a cost of $O(NF)$ for $F$ faces, and the closest points only for the few pairs within reach.
Whether the projection of a centre falls inside a face is decided by the even--odd crossing count of the polygon of the face, in an orthonormal frame of its plane.
Faces with fewer vertices than the largest one are padded with copies of their last vertex, so that all faces are processed together.
The redundant contacts at shared edges and vertices are found, for the particles that touch more than one face, by testing whether the closest point of one face lies on another.

\paragraph{Containment.}
Whether a point lies inside the polyhedron is decided by its winding number, the solid angle that the closed surface subtends at the point divided by $4\pi$: one inside and zero outside, whatever the shape of the polyhedron.
The solid angle of each face is the sum of those of the triangles of a fan from its first vertex, each evaluated in closed form~\cite{vanoosterom1983}.
The test runs at the escape checks, when particles are placed during deposition, and once when the porosity grid samples the domain.

\paragraph{Validation of the geometry.}
\texttt{Geometry3D} checks that every face is planar, that the faces close the polyhedron and are oriented alike, that the enclosed volume is not zero, that no two adjacent faces fold back onto each other, and that no edge of the polyhedron pierces a face.

\paragraph{Pore water.}
The porosity grid has cubic cells, the cloud-in-cell weights are trilinear over the eight nodes around a point, and the volume of the domain around each node is sampled with $8\times8\times8$ points per cell.
In the pressure equation~\eqref{eq:pressure-update} each node is a control volume of up to $h_{\varepsilon}^{3}$.
Two neighbouring nodes exchange water through a face of area $h_{\varepsilon}^{2}$ times its open fraction, so the conductance of a face is $(k/\mu_{w})\,h_{\varepsilon}$ times that fraction, and the carried flux crosses the same area.
Consider a column, drained at the top, whose skeleton carries water down onto an impermeable floor at a uniform rate $c$.
At steady state the Darcy flow returns the carried water, so the pressure below the drained node, at height $y_{d}$, is the linear profile $c\,(y_{d}-y)/(k/\mu_{w})$.
The solver reproduces it to a relative error below $10^{-6}$, also in the cells cut by the walls, and it reproduces the dissipation of a uniform excess pressure in such a column to within \num{1}\% of Terzaghi's solution, as in two dimensions.

\paragraph{Output.}
A frame stores the vertices of the faces of every loaded body.
\texttt{render\_animation} and \texttt{render\_frame} draw a three-dimensional simulation in an oblique orthographic view, with $y$ up, the particles to scale and coloured by their speed, the walls transparent and the loaded bodies on top.
Given a \texttt{section} coordinate they draw the vertical section $z=\text{const}$ instead, in which a sphere at a distance $\Delta z$ from the plane appears as a circle of radius $\sqrt{R^{2}-\Delta z^{2}}$.
The section of a domain extruded from a polygon is that polygon, so it looks like the output of the planar model.

\paragraph{Cost.}
The cost of a step remains essentially linear in $N$.
Filling a box of the same size takes many more particles in 3D than in 2D, though, and each particle has more neighbours.
A step of the square footing example, \texttt{examples/square\_footing\_3d.py}, with \num{552} particles in a box of ten faces, takes about \qty{2.2}{\milli\second}, and the whole example runs in under a minute.
